When a parent decides a child needs "help," the search almost always starts on Google. What they find there is not an accident. For decades the troubled teen industry (TTI) has worked to control what a worried parent sees: new names for programs with bad histories, "free help lines" that are really sales funnels, "independent" consultants paid by the programs they recommend, lawsuits against the people who post warnings, and brochures that promise a few months when the real stays run for years. This investigation sets out how it is done, using the industry's worst-documented offenders. Every example below comes from a court record, a government report or published reporting, and each one is linked in the sources at the end. Where something is only alleged in a lawsuit, we say so.

1. A new name for the same building

The cheapest way to bury bad search results is to stop being the thing people searched for. A program with a death, a lawsuit or a license revocation attached to its name can close, change hands or re-incorporate, and reopen under a name that has no history yet. A parent who searches the new name finds a clean website and nothing else.

Congress was told this in 2007. Investigating deaths and abuse in residential programs, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) found that five of the nine programs it looked at were "a permutation of the original program operating under a different name or in a new location." In one case the owner of a program, whose name was on Utah's child abuse registry, "opened and operated these programs elsewhere," and one of those programs was still "marketed on the Internet, along with 10 other programs." In another, an owner whose license was revoked after a child's death "applied for and received a new license to start a new program" sixteen months later.

The pattern did not stop in 2007:

  • Island View became Elevations RTC. Island View Residential Treatment Center in Syracuse, Utah, "technically closed in April 2014" and "was sold to a new company and renamed Elevations RTC," in the same building, KUER reported. Syracuse police calls to the facility became 40% more frequent after it reopened as Elevations. NBC News later reported that Family Help & Wellness had acquired Island View and renamed it. (See our Elevations RTC profile.)
  • Sequel became Vivant. The U.S. Senate Finance Committee found that in 2021, "after being plagued by reporting on abuse and neglect allegations, Sequel sold 13 facilities to a newly-incorporated company, Vivant," which was "also founded by Jay Ripley" and "retained many members of Sequel leadership." Vivant's leadership told Senate investigators "there was no public facing presence for the company." Public reporting, the committee noted, "alleged that this move to private ownership has allowed providers to escape liability," including for the 2020 death of Cornelius Fredericks at a Sequel facility in Michigan.
  • Trails Momentum became Grow at Momentum. North Carolina revoked Trails Carolina's license in October 2024, eight months after a 12-year-old boy died there; the medical examiner ruled the death a homicide by asphyxiation. Trails Carolina's management company, Wilderness Training & Consulting, also ran a program for young adults called Trails Momentum. In February 2025 the state told WLOS that "Momentum is not licensed" and opened a complaint investigation into whether it should be. By December 2025 the program had announced that it was "previously known as Trails Momentum" and was now "Grow at Momentum," dropping the word that ties it to Trails Carolina in a search. The program gave its own reasons for the change; the effect on what a parent finds is the same.

A change of owner is also used to disown a program's past. When Paris Hilton described the abuse she survived at Provo Canyon School, the school answered that it "was sold by its previous ownership in August 2000" and so "cannot comment on the operations or patient experience prior to that time." Universal Health Services, its owner, kept the name and the campus. Utah revoked the licenses of both Provo Canyon campuses in July 2026; the school said it disagreed with the decision.

This is why every facility record on this site carries its past and other names, and why the network map draws a line from each old name to the new one. Search the old names too.

2. "Help" that is really a sales funnel

Search for "help for my troubled teen" and many of the results are not programs at all. They are help lines, referral services, parent "advocates" and directories that look neutral and present themselves as a free guide through a hard decision. Many are paid by the programs they recommend.

WWASP and Teen Help. The World Wide Association of Specialty Programs and Schools (WWASP) was, in the words of a federal appeals court, an organization that "does not own or operate schools; rather, it markets its members' schools to parents." Its marketing arm, Teen Help, advertised to parents in magazines and online. Forbes reported in 1999 that an attorney suing the network called its corporate structure "extremely complex and convoluted -- deliberately so." A parent who called Teen Help was talking to the sales department of the schools it recommended.

PURE, the paid "parent advocate." Sue Scheff founded Parents Universal Resource Experts (PURE) less than a year after removing her daughter from a WWASP school, offering families information about programs; the schools it recommended competed with WWASP's. The Tenth Circuit's opinion in the case WWASP later brought against her sets out that "PURE schools pay Ms. Scheff a substantial sum whenever a child enrolls in its program based on her recommendation," and that "Ms. Scheff used various fictitious names to post negative messages about schools for at-risk teenagers on an internet forum." In other words, a referral broker paid per enrollment, presenting as a fellow parent, posting under made-up names to steer parents away from competitors. Scheff later co-wrote Google Bomb, a 2009 book about online reputation, with a foreword by the head of the reputation management company ReputationDefender.

What the GAO heard when it called. In 2008 GAO investigators posed as parents and called programs and referral services. One referral service's website promised, "We will look at your special situation and help you select the best school for your teen with individual attention." For three different fictional children with three different problems, it recommended the same Missouri boot camp. GAO found the service "is owned by the husband of the boot camp owner," and the callers were never told. Two other services presented themselves as separate businesses, "with separate names, Web sites, phone numbers, and magazine advertisements," but the receptionist at one answered the phone with the other's name. One referral agent advised the fictional father on what to tell his daughter about where she was going: "tell her it's a college prep boarding school."

Educational consultants. Families who can afford it are often told to hire an "educational consultant" to choose a program for them. In 2021 Oregon banned residential programs from paying consultants for referrals. The industry fought the bill, arguing that without the consultants the programs would go out of business, the Associated Press reported in 2026. Oregon state Sen. Sara Gelser Blouin, who has led the state's investigations into out-of-state placements, described the arrangement this way: "you go to the consultant and $10,000 later, they make a recommendation ... It's this whole big racket." (Some program operators deny paying consultants. Ask any consultant, in writing, whether they or their firm receive anything from the programs they recommend.)

Adult addiction treatment had the same problem, and regulators acted. After The Verge exposed rehab "help lines" and review sites that were secretly marketing operations, Google restricted rehab advertising in 2017 and began requiring treatment advertisers to be certified by LegitScript. In 2022 the Federal Trade Commission brought its first case under a new law against a rehab referral service, R360, which claimed to match people to centers "selected through a rigorous evaluation process conducted by an expert." No equivalent rule exists for programs that take children. We could find no Google advertising policy, certification or federal law that applies specifically to troubled teen referral sites.

3. Suing the people who post warnings

When the results a parent finds are critical, the industry has gone after the people who wrote them. Most of these suits failed in court. They did not need to win to do damage: a lawsuit costs a survivor, a parent or a reporter years and money they rarely have, and it teaches everyone watching to be careful what they post. Lawsuits meant to silence critics rather than to win are called SLAPPs (strategic lawsuits against public participation), and many states now let a defendant get them thrown out early.

  • WWASP v. PURE (Sue Scheff and Jeff Berryman). WWASP sued Scheff, whose service steered families to competing schools, for defamation, conspiracy, interference and unfair competition. The court ended the claims against Berryman before trial; a jury found for Scheff on every count in 2004. The Tenth Circuit affirmed in 2006, holding that WWASP was a public figure on the subject and had to prove actual malice.
  • WWASP v. Thomas Houlahan. WWASP sued a UPI reporter who was researching abuse at its schools. The suit was dismissed, and the dismissal was affirmed in 2005.
  • Robert Lichfield v. Shelby Earnshaw and ISAC. WWASP's founder sued Earnshaw and the International Survivors Action Committee, which had reported abuse at WWASP schools to state officials, for defamation, invasion of privacy and interference with his "prospective economic advantage," the Deseret News reported in 2005. Earnshaw denied spreading untruths.
  • Diamond Ranch Academy v. Chelsea Filer. Diamond Ranch Academy in Utah sued a former student over what the court called her "campaign (mostly on the Internet)," including the Facebook page "I Survived Diamond Ranch Academy." In 2016 the court threw out part of the suit under California's anti-SLAPP law and let other claims go forward. In 2023 Utah ordered Diamond Ranch Academy closed after a child died there.
  • Narvin Lichfield v. Netflix and Katherine Kubler. In 2024 Narvin Lichfield, brother of Robert Lichfield and a figure in WWASP's history, sued over the Netflix series The Program: Cons, Cults, and Kidnapping, in which survivors of the Academy at Ivy Ridge describe what was done to them. He asked the court for damages and for the "defamatory and disparaging" content to be removed. The court dismissed the suit under Utah's and California's laws protecting public expression, and in July 2026 the Tenth Circuit affirmed: "A person who is the subject of the speech is not entitled to hold the editor's pen or exercise a producer's review merely because he is the subject of a given segment or article."

The same tool was used inside the industry. Scheff, the paid "advocate" above, won an $11.3 million default verdict in 2006 against a mother who had called her a "con artist" on the survivor forum Fornits. The case is still cited as a warning about posting online.

4. Discredit the witnesses, and leave no paper trail

Survivors are the industry's biggest reputation problem, because their accounts are what a parent finds when they search. The standard answer is to say they cannot be believed. In a 2022 statement quoted by NBC News, Family Help & Wellness and Elevations RTC said former clients who criticize the programs "are unreliable narrators who mischaracterize their treatment," and that children are safer in their programs than in public schools. The people being called unreliable are the people who were there.

The trade groups have also worked to keep problems out of writing. In October 2020, while Utah's Disability Law Center was investigating programs, the executive director of the National Association of Therapeutic Schools and Programs (NATSAP) told roughly 130 members on a call: "With the DLC being active, I would ask that if people have questions about this to call me... Let's not email." KUER obtained the recording. (Read more in our NATSAP investigation.)

5. Selling a brochure the records contradict

The websites parents find say what parents want to hear. The records say something else.

  • Length of stay. The Senate Finance Committee compared what the largest companies told parents with their own data. UHS's Natchez Trace Youth Academy said "stays are typically three to nine months." Devereux advertised "approximately four to six months," and Acadia Healthcare "several weeks or months." Yet 135 Devereux programs had average stays of more than a year, one Devereux stay lasted 6,928 days, about 19 years, and in 2022 forty UHS facilities had at least one stay of more than a year. The committee concluded that "providers only advertise short to medium duration stays on their websites."
  • "Inspected." In GAO's 2008 calls, a Texas wilderness program told the caller that NATSAP "absolutely" inspects its members. GAO: "NATSAP does not perform inspections of its member programs." The same program said its school credits were "fully transferable" and that insurance would pay. Membership badges, accreditation seals and "licensed" banners on a program's website say little about what an inspector has found; the state's own reports, where they exist, say more. We collect them at Inspection Reports.
  • "Research shows." Wilderness programs often point to studies showing that their treatment works. Undark reported in 2020 that the research center producing most of those studies had received at least $1 million from the industry's trade group, the Outdoor Behavioral Healthcare Council, since 1999, that all seven of its researchers had worked in or had strong ties to the industry, and that the studies appeared mainly in journals founded by related trade groups. After the reporting, the center added a statement naming the OBH Council and NATSAP as its funders.
  • Care that was advertised and not delivered. In 2020 UHS paid $122 million to settle U.S. Justice Department allegations that its facilities, among other things, induced admissions with free transportation and "did not provide the level of care it advertised." UHS did not admit the allegations. Parents of children at Trails Carolina and its sister programs have since sued their management company alleging false advertising, and in 2025 four former residents filed a federal suit alleging they were sold "industry-leading therapy" by programs run as "cash machines" for private equity. Those claims have not been decided.

6. What we could not prove yet

Survivors and parents describe more than we can document here. We have looked for reliable evidence of each of the following and have not found a court, regulator or newsroom that has established it for a named program, so we do not name anyone:

  • fake or staff-written Google and Facebook reviews, reviews paid for with discounts, and "review gating" (asking only happy families to post);
  • "Is [program] a scam? Honest reviews" blog posts and press releases written by reputation management firms to push critical results off the first page;
  • takedown notices (copyright or "privacy" claims) sent to remove survivors' posts, and edits to Wikipedia articles by people paid by programs;
  • enrollment contracts that forbid families from criticizing a program in public.

If you have evidence of any of these, such as an email asking you for a review, a contract clause, a takedown notice or an invoice from a reputation firm, please send it to us. You can contact us or upload documents through our anonymous document submission page, which encrypts what you send.

How to read the results you find

  • Search every name. Find out what the program used to be called, who owns it and what else the owner runs. Our program index and network map list past names and parent companies.
  • Ask who pays the person helping you. A free help line, directory or "parent advocate" is paid by someone. Ask in writing whether they receive anything from any program, and how much.
  • Read the state's reports, not the seals. Membership in a trade group is not an inspection. Look up the program's licensing and inspection history, starting at our Inspection Reports.
  • Believe the survivors over the brochure. Look for what former residents say on their own sites and forums. A program that calls its former students liars is telling you something.
  • Ask for the numbers. Ask for the average and longest length of stay in writing, and compare them with what the website says.
