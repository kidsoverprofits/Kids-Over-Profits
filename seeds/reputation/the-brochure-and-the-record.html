Program websites tell parents what they want to hear: a few months away, licensed and inspected, backed by research. Government investigators who compared those promises with the programs' own records found something else.

Part of our series How the Troubled Teen Industry Manages Its Reputation. Every example comes from a court record, a government report, the company's own words or published reporting, and each is linked in the sources at the end. Where something is only alleged in a lawsuit, we say so.

What the websites say, and what the records say

The websites parents find say what parents want to hear. The records say something else.

  • Length of stay. The Senate Finance Committee compared what the largest companies told parents with their own data. UHS's Natchez Trace Youth Academy said "stays are typically three to nine months." Devereux advertised "approximately four to six months," and Acadia Healthcare "several weeks or months." Yet 135 Devereux programs had average stays of more than a year, one Devereux stay lasted 6,928 days, about 19 years, and in 2022 forty UHS facilities had at least one stay of more than a year. The committee concluded that "providers only advertise short to medium duration stays on their websites."
  • "Inspected." In GAO's 2008 calls, a Texas wilderness program told the caller that NATSAP "absolutely" inspects its members. GAO: "NATSAP does not perform inspections of its member programs." The same program said its school credits were "fully transferable" and that insurance would pay. Membership badges, accreditation seals and "licensed" banners on a program's website say little about what an inspector has found; the state's own reports, where they exist, say more. We collect them at Inspection Reports.
  • "Research shows." Wilderness programs often point to studies showing that their treatment works. Undark reported in 2020 that the research center producing most of those studies had received at least $1 million from the industry's trade group, the Outdoor Behavioral Healthcare Council, since 1999, that all seven of its researchers had worked in or had strong ties to the industry, and that the studies appeared mainly in journals founded by related trade groups. After the reporting, the center added a statement naming the OBH Council and NATSAP as its funders.
  • Care that was advertised and not delivered. In 2020 UHS paid $122 million to settle U.S. Justice Department allegations that its facilities, among other things, induced admissions with free transportation and "did not provide the level of care it advertised." UHS did not admit the allegations. Parents of children at Trails Carolina and its sister programs have since sued their management company alleging false advertising, and in 2025 four former residents filed a federal suit alleging they were sold "industry-leading therapy" by programs run as "cash machines" for private equity. Those claims have not been decided.
