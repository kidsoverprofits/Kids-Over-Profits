Every troubled teen program sells the same few things: that your child will be safe, watched, treated by licensed professionals and kept close to the family. These are the promises a frightened parent pays for, and they are the ones the records break. The children who died in these programs in the last five years died at exactly the point the website said was covered.

What we found

  • Thirteen days before Taylor Goodridge died of an untreated infection, Diamond Ranch Academy's website promised that "if your child becomes ill or injured, medical staff will attend to their needs." She vomited at least 14 times in her last 12 days, and no one took her to a hospital until the day she died.
  • Vive Adolescent Care called itself "a medical-driven model based upon safety." Arianna Duenez lay dead for up to ten hours while staff signed off checks that were never done. The state fined Vive $1,000.
  • In March 2019 Sequel Youth and Family Services announced a "restraint-free" program. Thirteen months later, staff at its Lakeside Academy held 16-year-old Cornelius Fredericks down for throwing bread. He died two days later.
  • Trails Carolina promised "trained staff supervision and access to medical professionals at all times." Clark Harman, 12, suffocated zipped inside a bivy sack on his first night, while the staffer assigned to check on him did not.
  • New Lifehouse Academy advertised "licensed counseling." It has no licensed counselors, The Frontier found, and added a disclaimer to its home page only after reporters asked.

Part of our series How the Troubled Teen Industry Manages Its Reputation. Each program's claim is quoted from its own website as it stood before the events described, from a copy we keep; each record comes from a state inspection, a court filing, a government report or published reporting, linked in the sources at the end. Where something is only alleged in a lawsuit, we say so.

"Medical staff will attend to their needs"

Diamond Ranch Academy in Hurricane, Utah, told parents its medical department was made up of "experienced, licensed medical professionals whose mission is to provide the highest quality of care to each and every student," and that "it is our intent and goal to treat every student as if they were our own son or daughter." Its FAQ explained that medical staff were on campus from 6 a.m. to 9 p.m., with a doctor, a nurse practitioner and nurses "on call 24/7" (our copy, our copy). Both pages were captured on December 7, 2022.

The same page held the catch: "students are required to take initiative and accountability for their own healthcare," down to "requesting appointments." Thirteen days later, 17-year-old Taylor Goodridge died of peritonitis. "In the 12-day period prior to the client's death, program documentation recorded that the client vomited at least 14 times," Utah's Department of Health and Human Services found. The school did not try to take her to a hospital until the day she died, NBC News reported. Her family's lawyer says staff had been "begging" management to take her for days. Utah refused to renew the license in 2023.

Vive Adolescent Care in St. George called itself "A Medical-Driven Model, Based Upon Safety." "Our medical team completes daily protocols that surpass all standard evaluation requirements," its site said in May 2023, and "the focus is primarily on physical safety" (our copy, our copy). Five weeks later, 16-year-old Arianna Duenez died there. Staff were supposed to check every 15 minutes that she was breathing; the state found they had "signed off on those checks as if they'd been performed," and police estimated she had been dead for four and a half to ten hours, FOX 13 reported. The state fined Vive $1,000. Vive is still open, and today its instructions to AI chatbots tell them not to use FOX 13, the station that reported her death (see Telling the Chatbots What to Say).

Maple Lake Academy in Spanish Fork promised "a safe and supportive framework," "an intensive, individualized program," and a psychiatrist on campus "every other week and more often when needed" (our copy, our copy). Sofia Soto, 14, died there in January 2022. The state's notice: "After repeated requests from parents, the program took the client to be evaluated by a doctor. After the doctor visit, the client complained of worsening symptoms but was not returned for medical care." Her mother had texted the program: "Sofi is definitely not well."

Three Utah programs in eighteen months. Each sold medical care as the reason to trust it, and each left a sick girl without it.

"Supervision at all times"

"Wilderness therapy programs are carefully designed to minimize risk to all participants, with trained staff supervision and access to medical professionals at all times," Trails Carolina wrote in 2023, under a post debunking "myths" about wilderness therapy. Its page for boys 10 to 13 promised "a safe, healthy environment" and techniques that put "student safety" first (our copy, our copy).

Clark Harman was 12. On his first night, in February 2024, he was zipped into a bivy sack with an alarm, used, the district attorney said, "to prevent a minor from running away during the night." A staff member was supposed to check on him at midnight, 3 a.m. and 6 a.m. "My actions that night was to perform night checks … that was my responsibility, which I failed on," that staffer told the state. The medical examiner ruled the death a homicide by "asphyxia due to smothering." North Carolina revoked the license. The district attorney brought no charges.

The company behind Trails, Family Help & Wellness, also ran Asheville Academy for Girls. In March 2025 its site promised "care and guidance 24/7 in all environments," staff chosen through "rigorous interview and screening processes," and accreditation "which many believe is the gold standard" (our copy). In May two girls, 14 and 12, died there by suicide, 26 days apart. The state found the program "failed to provide appropriate structure, adult supervision and control," that a staff member on duty when the first girl died had no CPR or first aid certification, and that the only medication-trained worker on the shift was not current in CPR either. Asheville Academy surrendered its license in June 2025 with $45,000 in penalties.

Discovery Ranch in Mapleton, Utah, offered "round-the-clock support" that "creates a protective environment for teenagers during difficult times" (our copy, captured October 4, 2024). A month later, 17-year-old Biruk Silvers died by suicide there. The state found "that a client who had expressed suicidal ideation and intent was not provided with the level of supervision needed to keep them safe," and that "the provider did not follow their suicide prevention policy." Utah put the license on conditional status. In January inspectors at its sister program, Discovery Ranch South, found a staff member asleep "with an open bottle of pills that spilled in their lap and on the floor."

"Restraint-free"

On March 6, 2019, Sequel Youth and Family Services announced it had adopted a "restraint-free behavior management program," piloted at its Clarinda academy, and expected "to continue rolling it out to our other residential treatment programs." "Sequel is committed to creating and maintaining a safe, trusting, and caring environment," its chief operating officer said (our copy). Its page for Lakeside Academy in Kalamazoo advertised accreditation and "24 Hour Supervision" (our copy).

On April 29, 2020, staff at Lakeside restrained 16-year-old Cornelius Fredericks after he threw a piece of bread. "Throwing bread is not a demonstration of imminent threat of harm to self or others," Michigan's investigators wrote; another 12 minutes went by before anyone sought medical help or started CPR. He died two days later, and his death was ruled a homicide. Michigan revoked the license. Three staff members were charged; two pleaded no contest and were sentenced to probation. Sequel closed or sold its programs, and its co-founder formed a new company, Vivant, that bought back 13 of them, as New Names for the Same Programs shows. Sequel's restraint-free promise lasted thirteen months.

"Licensed counseling"

New Lifehouse Academy, a Teen Challenge program for girls in Eucha, Oklahoma, offers to take girls who are "Depressed, withdrawn or self-destructive." In January 2026 its site listed "Licensed counseling and behavioral therapy tailored to adolescent girls," and called it "a safe, nurturing place where a troubled teen can heal" (our copy).

"The program doesn't need a state license to operate and has no licensed counselors," The Frontier reported in September 2026. Staff told one family it had "life coaches and student mentors" instead; some of the coaches were the program's own graduates. Its 2025 handbook told parents not to believe their children's complaints. Only after The Frontier contacted it did New Lifehouse add a line to its home page saying it "is not licensed to provide clinical treatment for youth with mental health disorders." Two former students are suing; one alleges that staff "would violently restrain young girls who broke the rules," while the program "markets that it does not condone shaming or isolation." Those claims have not been decided.

Resolution Ranch Academy in Cameron, Texas, describes "Licensed individual, group, and family therapy" in "a safe, nature-rich rural (ranch) environment" (our copy). Its own introduction packet for parents shows what the structure is (our copy): a "Talk Ban" in which "your child is prohibited from talking"; a "Room Ban" in which "you must sit awake on your bed with no one else around except for a direct care worker"; a "Code Red" that puts "the entire camp" on "an isolation status" until "attitudes are brought into line"; no visits for the first 30 days, then one 10-minute phone call a week. When a worker hands out a punishment, "the proper response is 'Thank you.'"

Texas inspectors found that "a staff did not follow protocol for restraints and things escalated" (2022), that three children "have seen the same staff member fall asleep during night shift" (2024), and that "a child in care attempted suicide and licensing was not notified of the incident" (2024).

"Reunite them with their family"

"Since its founding, the idea of family has remained a foundational precept of the program," Turning Winds in Troy, Montana, tells parents; its mission is to "reunite them with their family" (our copy). Montana's surveyors kept finding the family left out. In 2022, case plans for six of nine children reviewed had no record that the child or a parent took part in writing them. In 2023 a serious incident report had no record that the parent was told within 24 hours. In 2024 the program again had no record that parents had reviewed and signed their children's plans.

"Three to nine months"

The largest companies make the same promises at scale. In 2024 the Senate Finance Committee set what they told parents beside their own data. UHS's Natchez Trace Youth Academy said "stays are typically three to nine months"; Devereux advertised "approximately four to six months." Yet 135 Devereux programs had average stays of more than a year, one Devereux stay lasted 6,928 days, about 19 years, and in 2022 forty UHS facilities had at least one stay of more than a year. "Providers only advertise short to medium duration stays on their websites," the committee concluded.

UHS had already paid $122 million in 2020 to settle Justice Department allegations that its facilities, among other things, "did not provide the level of care it advertised." It did not admit them.

"The least intrusive"

The Judge Rotenberg Center in Canton, Massachusetts, calls its treatment "the least intrusive, most effective form of treatment" and promises that "once we accept a student we do not expel him/her" (our copy). Its 2018 brochure mentions dress codes, a token-operated TV and "devices to prevent self-injury" (our copy). Neither names the electric skin-shock devices it uses on its students. In 2024 the FDA proposed banning them for the second time, finding that they "present an unreasonable and substantial risk of illness or injury," and that the center is the only facility in the country using them.

The seal on the page

Trails Carolina told parents that CARF accreditation "requires extremely high standards for safety"; Asheville Academy called it "the gold standard." Discovery Ranch and Lakeside Academy advertised Joint Commission accreditation. Every one of those seals was on the program's website in the months before a child died there. An accreditor is paid by the program it accredits and sees what the program shows it; the state reports above were written by people who came to investigate a death. Where those reports exist, we collect them at Inspection Reports.

None of these promises was unusual. They are the industry's standard copy: safety, supervision, licensed care, family. What made these programs different is that a child died or a reporter looked, and the record was written down. Brochures often cite research, too; "Research Shows": The Industry's Own Studies looks at who paid for it.
