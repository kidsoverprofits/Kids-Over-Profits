Troubled teen programs have sold themselves to frightened parents for fifty years: in magazine ads, mailed brochures, admissions packets and now websites. We keep about 90 of them in our document library, from the 1990s to 2021.

The method barely changes. Frighten the parent, describe the child as the problem, show the scenery instead of the results, and send everything to an intake line. Then compare the brochure with the record.

What we found

  • A "public service" booklet lists 34 "warning signs" of drug use, among them "Depression" and "Defiles Family Values." It ends with a toll-free number and links to the programs that paid for it.
  • Spring Creek Lodge's ad offered a "WARRANTY." A 16-year-old killed herself there, and Montana created its licensing board afterward.
  • A Texas ranch's packet for parents describes a "Talk Ban," a "Room Ban" in which a boy "must sit awake on your bed," and a "Code Red" that puts the whole camp "on an isolation status."
  • The Judge Rotenberg Center's brochure calls its treatment "the least intrusive." It never mentions the electric skin shocks the FDA has twice moved to ban.
  • Websites advertise stays of a few months. The Senate found Devereux programs averaging more than a year.

Part of our series How the Troubled Teen Industry Manages Its Reputation. Every example comes from a court record, a government report, the company's own words or published reporting, and each is linked in the sources at the end. Where something is only alleged in a lawsuit, we say so.

Fear first

"Nothing is more destructive"

In 2001 the Parent Resources Hotline offered a "free booklet," How To Tell If Your Teen Is Using Drugs. It opens: "Nothing is more destructive to a teen and/or their family than the use of drugs" (Wayback Machine, our copy).

Its "Thirty Four WARNING Signs Of Drug Use" include "Poor self image," "Grades dropping," "Change in friends," "Depression," "Withdrawal" and "Disrespectful to parents." Some are judgments, not signs: "Defiles Family Values," "Manipulative/Self-Centered," "Sneaky behavior." Nearly any parent of a teenager would recognize several.

Parents who hesitate are warned that "silently clinging to the denial and hope that these issues will not be part of their children's lives can create an equally devastating consequence."

"This free booklet is offered as a Public Service," the last page says, above a toll-free number and links to Tranquility Bay, Casa by the Sea, Carolina Springs Academy, Spring Creek Lodge and Cross Creek Manor. All were programs of the World Wide Association of Specialty Programs and Schools (WWASP).

The same number, 800-793-5156, is printed as "PARENT RESOURCES" on Spring Creek Lodge's ad (our copy).

The child as the problem

Cherokee Creek Boys School in South Carolina takes boys aged 11 to 15. Its 2021 fact sheet lists what it works with, including "High-Functioning Autism Spectrum," "Adoption," "Bullying" and "Technology Overuse." The average stay is "15 – 24 months" (our copy).

Tuition was $5,825 a month in 2008, "plus additional fees for psychotherapy" (our copy). By 2021 it was $12,500 a month. At that rate, an average stay costs $187,500 to $300,000. The only evidence offered is to put families "in touch with both alumni and current" parents.

Selling the place, not the results

"A Paradise For Change In Costa Rica!"

WWASP's Academy at Dundee Ranch sold distance from home as treatment: "Finding themselves in entirely new surroundings, teens tend to be much more teachable and open to new direction" (our copy).

In May 2003 a Costa Rican prosecutor raided the ranch. Costa Rica's child welfare office "promptly closed the school, citing reports of abuse and saying the adolescents were being held against their will, which is illegal in Costa Rica," the Tico Times reported. Nearly three dozen of the 200 students ran away during a riot.

The owner, Narvin Lichfield, was briefly detained and charged. Judges acquitted him in 2007 for lack of evidence, the Tico Times later reported.

"WARRANTY PROVIDED"

Spring Creek Lodge Academy in Thompson Falls, Montana, advertised "WARRANTY PROVIDED. Call for details." Its ad promised that "inappropriate choices are confronted, consequented, and redirected" (our copy).

One testimonial in it begins: "My daughter was escorted to your Program at the age of 13."

In 2004, 16-year-old Karlye Newman killed herself at Spring Creek Lodge. Montana's oversight board for private teen programs "was established after a 16-year-old girl killed herself at Spring Creek Lodge," the Missoulian reported in 2019, and "the board's majority comes from the industry it regulates." By law, three of its five members came from "a list of nominees provided by programs."

Ranch work for third graders

Majestic Ranch Academy in Utah, another WWASP program, took children in "3rd through 9th grade." They helped "with calving, lambing, caring for cattle and sheep" (our copy). One testimonial: "I am now a law abiding, handsome, loving, pure, divine young man."

What "structure" means

Few brochures say what happens when a child breaks a rule. Resolution Ranch in Cameron, Texas, wrote it down in its 2018 introduction packet for parents (our copy).

  • Zeros. "Each zero equates to 45 minutes of extra chores on Saturday." When a worker gives one, "the proper response is 'Thank you.'"
  • Talk Ban. "Talk Ban means exactly what it sounds like—your child is prohibited from talking."
  • Room Ban. "You must sit awake on your bed with no one else around except for a direct care worker."
  • Code Red. "The entire camp is placed on an isolation status," lifted "when order has been restored and attitudes are brought into line."
  • Writing. A boy who wastes food is assigned "a 5-page report on Starvation and Malnutrition."
  • Contact. "No visits will be allowed during the resident's first 30 days." Then phone calls "limited to one 10-minute call per week."
  • Silence among new boys. They may not communicate with each other, including "smiling, gesturing, eye signals."

Chrysalis, a girls' program in Montana, tells an incoming girl, "We are a family focused program." The same sheet says she will talk with her family in weekly family therapy and that "a few months after arriving you will also have social calls with your family" (our copy).

What the brochure leaves out

The Judge Rotenberg Center

The center's 2018 brochure describes dress codes and a token-operated TV, and says its Electronics Department has built "devices to prevent self-injury" (our copy). Its marketing slides call it "the least intrusive, most effective form of treatment" and promise that "once we accept a student we do not expel him/her" (our copy).

Neither names the electric skin-shock devices the center uses. In 2024 the FDA proposed banning them for a second time, finding that they "present an unreasonable and substantial risk of illness or injury," and that the Judge Rotenberg Center is the only facility in the country using them. A federal appeals court had struck down the first ban in 2021.

Trails Carolina

A company timeline from Family Help & Wellness says Trails Carolina "has pioneered what we have all come to recognize as outdoor therapy" and is "the leading wilderness therapy program on the east coast" (our copy).

On February 3, 2024, 12-year-old Clark Harman was found dead at Trails Carolina, less than a day after he arrived. The medical examiner ruled the death a homicide, by "asphyxia due to smothering," NBC News reported. North Carolina revoked the program's license.

Sequel Youth & Family Services

In 2013 South Dakota's Department of Corrections chose Sequel to run a program for youths. Sequel's description in the state's slides offers one qualification: it "has experienced tremendous growth" (our copy). Its list of programs includes Lakeside Academy in Kalamazoo, Michigan.

In April 2020 staff at Lakeside restrained 16-year-old Cornelius Fredericks (spelled Frederick in some reports) after he threw a sandwich. He died two days later; the medical examiner ruled the death a homicide caused by "restraint asphyxia," CBS News reported. Michigan revoked the license after finding 10 violations.

"This is a growth industry"

In 2002 Forbes profiled the Brown Schools, owner of CEDU (our copy). Its chief executive, Marguerite Sallee, "talks about cross-selling therapeutic programs as if they were software packages." "This is a growth industry," she said.

The same issue advised parents to hire a transport escort, "a 'friendly gorilla' in the trade," and reported that at CEDU "one in four students quits" before graduating. Three years later the Brown Schools filed for bankruptcy and its schools closed at once, the Spokesman-Review reported.

A directory nobody checked

A 2008–2009 national directory of Christian residential homes for children lists hundreds of programs. Its compiler writes: "I have not examined each home and in no way want to communicate that we are giving a stamp of approval to any ministry" (our copy).

It marks as "SECURE" "those places that would hold a youth against their will," and, for a child who resists placement, suggests parents "consider an escort service."

Promises and records

The websites parents find say what parents want to hear. The records say something else.

Rep. George Miller summed it up when he opened the House hearing on deceptive marketing in 2008: programs "claim to be subject to independent inspections that never happen. They claim to offer services that they don't, like schooling with transferable education credits. They assure parents that health insurance will cover the cost of their services when, in reality, it won't."

Length of stay

The Senate Finance Committee compared what the largest companies told parents with their own data.

UHS's Natchez Trace Youth Academy said "stays are typically three to nine months." Devereux advertised "approximately four to six months," and Acadia Healthcare "several weeks or months." Yet 135 Devereux programs had average stays of more than a year, one Devereux stay lasted 6,928 days, about 19 years, and in 2022 forty UHS facilities had at least one stay of more than a year.

The committee concluded that "providers only advertise short to medium duration stays on their websites."

"Inspected."

In GAO's 2008 calls, a Texas wilderness program told the caller that NATSAP "absolutely" inspects its members. GAO: "NATSAP does not perform inspections of its member programs." The same program said its school credits were "fully transferable" and that insurance would pay.

Membership badges, accreditation seals and "licensed" banners on a program's website say little about what an inspector has found; the state's own reports, where they exist, say more. We collect them at Inspection Reports.

"Experienced staff."

Cynthia and Michael Harvey chose Catherine Freer Wilderness for their 15-year-old daughter, Erica, Cynthia Harvey told Congress in 2007, "because they have claimed to be fully licensed, because they were JCAHO accredited, because they claimed experience with teens being treated with psychiatric medications." They were told their daughter would be treated by experienced staff, therapists, wilderness guides and emergency medical technicians.

Erica died of heat stroke on May 2, 2002, the first full day of her trek. Afterward, her mother said, they learned that "none of the Freer team had experience with administering psychotropic drugs," that the EMT "was on his very first trek," and that the on-call doctor "turned out not to be a doctor at all, rather a physician's assistant located in Oregon."

Care that was advertised and not delivered

In 2020 UHS paid $122 million to settle U.S. Justice Department allegations that its facilities, among other things, induced admissions with free transportation and "did not provide the level of care it advertised." UHS did not admit the allegations.

Parents of children at Trails Carolina and its sister programs have since sued their management company alleging false advertising, and in 2025 four former residents filed a federal suit alleging they were sold "industry-leading therapy" by programs run as "cash machines" for private equity. Those claims have not been decided.

"Research shows"

Brochures often cite research. Our article "Research Shows": The Industry's Own Studies looks at who paid for it, who published it, and what its tables say.

A fairer example

Not every brochure is like these. Boys Town's admissions brochure is careful and cites its own long-term research (our copy). Even so, its headline is about the children who finish: "Kids that complete our program are succeeding." It does not say how many leave early, or how they do.
