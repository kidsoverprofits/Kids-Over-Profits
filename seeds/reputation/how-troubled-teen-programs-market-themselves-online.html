A worried parent looking for help for a child almost always starts with a search engine. What they find there has been built for them. Programs and the marketing companies they hire publish thousands of near-identical pages so that a search from any town turns up a program hundreds of miles away; "independent" directories and free help lines turn out to be run or paid by the programs they recommend; and consultants are paid for the placements they advise on. This article sets out how it works, using the programs that do it most.

What we found

  • Turning Winds publishes more than 2,500 pages, one for nearly every town, each calling its Montana campus a "sober living home" for that town.
  • Two "independent" directories belong to one marketing company, Exceed Marketing Solutions, which also builds the websites of the programs they recommend.
  • The directory's help line is "sponsored by schools and programs." Programs it features include Vive, where a 16-year-old died, and New Lifehouse, the subject of two federal lawsuits.
  • Parents read a trade group's badge as accreditation, Congress was told in 2007. It is not one.
  • The same companies now write instructions for AI chatbots too: see Telling the Chatbots What to Say.

Part of our series How the Troubled Teen Industry Manages Its Reputation. Every example comes from a court record, a government report, the company's own words or published reporting, and each is linked in the sources at the end. Where something is only alleged in a lawsuit, we say so.

A page for every town

A worried parent rarely searches for a program by name. They search for "help for troubled teens in Ohio," "military school near me," "group homes in Naperville." A program in rural Montana or southern Utah cannot be in all of those places, so many programs publish a page for each of them instead: the same few paragraphs, again and again, with the name of the town or state swapped in.

Google calls this "doorway abuse" and lists it as spam. Its examples include "having multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page" and "creating substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy."

We counted these pages on programs' websites using the lists of pages each site publishes for search engines (its sitemap), on October 1, 2026:

Turning Winds

(Troy, Montana): more than 2,500 pages for individual towns and cities, in four families: "group homes in [city]," "behavior modification programs [city]," "adolescent treatment programs for teens in [city] that take insurance," and "[city] sober living home for troubled teens." Two of these pages, for Agoura, California and Allen, Texas, are 84% word-for-word identical.

Each tells the parent that Turning Winds is a "sober living home," and each admits, "Although Turning Winds may be located outside the area of Agoura, CA, Turning Winds accepts troubled teens from all over the country." A search for the program's reviews leads to turningwindsreviews.com, registered in September 2025, which speaks in the program's own voice ("our unique therapeutic program") and shows only "5-star reviews."

What those pages leave out, the Missoulian reported in 2019: Turning Winds began in Idaho, where in 2005 the state gave its directors 72 hours' notice that its license would be suspended after John, John Jr. and Carl Baisden were cited for "injury to a child." The charges were later dropped, and the family sued over the prosecution.

The program moved to Montana the following year; Owen Baisden told the paper the timing was "coincidental."

Evangelhouse

(St. Martinville, Louisiana): nearly 900 pages naming a state, about eighteen for each of the fifty: "[state] trauma treatment center for girls," "[state] treatment center for defiant girls," "[state] self-harm treatment center for girls," and "[state] victims of sexual exploitation treatment."

Seven Stars, Elevations RTC, ViewPoint Center and Solstice RTC

(Utah): their main websites carry almost no location pages, but each runs a second website hidden under the same address (at /b/) with its own sitemap. Seven Stars' holds 407 state pages, such as "Asperger's boarding school" in each state; Elevations' 310, such as "adolescent psychiatric programs" in Boston and Beverly Hills; ViewPoint's about 150, including a "mental health summer camp" page for each state, from a psychiatric assessment center.

These programs, formerly owned by Family Help & Wellness, announced in 2026 that they had joined EOSIS, a company owned by the private equity firm Renovus Capital Partners.

Stillwater Academy

(South Jordan, Utah): 385 of the 417 pages on its site name a state or city, such as "troubled teen boarding school in Aurora."

Liahona Academy

(Hurricane, Utah): about 360 state pages, including "boot camps in [state] for troubled teens" and "military schools in [state] for troubled teens" for nearly every state. Liahona is neither a boot camp nor a military school.

The pages catch the parent who is searching for one and tell them, as its Texas page does, that "Liahona Academy is often the best option for Texas parents who think that military schools in Texas for troubled youth are their only choice."

The directories that are really one marketing company

The pages do not have to sit on a program's own website. MilitarySchoolUSA.com has more than 1,200 pages, nearly all of them for a state or a city, such as "Military Schools in Ohio for Troubled Teens."

A parent who lands on one is told that "most military schools in Ohio will not enroll teens who are struggling with inappropriate behavior," and that a therapeutic boarding school "may be a superior choice," with a free help line: "Military schools don't enroll troubled teens... My assistance is without obligation or cost."

BestTherapeuticBoardingSchools.com calls itself "an independent resource" and "an independent informational directory," with more than 300 pages for states and cities.

Its California page offers "programs and schools near California"; every program it links to is in another state, mostly Missouri, Utah and Oklahoma.

Both sites are copyrighted to the same company, Exceed Marketing Solutions, LLC, which describes itself as "a leading marketing partner for boarding schools and adolescent therapeutic programs, helping them achieve and maintain full enrollment."

Exceed's website says "Exceed Clients Are Listed in Our Own Highly Ranked Boarding School & Rehab Directories," that "participation with Exceed includes free featured listings in our highly ranked directories," and that "the directories it operates help quickly boost clients to prominence in the search engines." It names 21 of them, among them besttherapeuticboardingschools.com, militaryschoolusa.com, therapeuticboardingschools.org, troubledteenschools.us, rescueyouth.com, educationalconsultants.co and teenchallengeschools.com.

It also "manages ancillary sites, social media, directory listings, hosting and email accounts" for its clients, and the websites of most of the programs the "independent" directory features load files from Exceed's own folders.

One testimonial on Exceed's site comes from a former director of Brush Creek Academy and New Lifehouse Academy, two Teen Challenge of Oklahoma programs the directory features: "since we began using them we have not had a month go by that our beds were not full."

The directory's help line belongs to Best Choice Admissions Services, which shares a Spanish-language phone number with the Exceed directories.

On its own site Best Choice says, in capitals, that it "is sponsored by schools and programs, therefore our help is offered at no charge to families," and that "we are compensated by participating schools." On BestTherapeuticBoardingSchools.com, the same arrangement reads: "We're able to provide this help at no cost to you because the schools and programs we work with value the care we take in matching families."

This is the arrangement GAO found in 2008: referral services that "represent themselves as separate entities, with separate names, Web sites, phone numbers," steering families to the programs that pay them.

Here is some of what the directory does not tell parents about the programs it features:

Vive Adolescent Care

(St. George, Utah): 16-year-old Arianna Duenez died there on July 2, 2023. FOX13 reported that for more than an hour and a half before she was found, no one checked on her, though staff signed off the checks as done. The state fined Vive $1,000, put its psychiatric program on a conditional license and temporarily banned admissions.

New Lifehouse Academy

(Oklahoma): The Frontier interviewed 24 former students in 2026; 13 described staff using physical restraint holds, including a long-time staff member who "routinely restrained girls by sitting on their legs and holding them down." After The Frontier's questions, the program added a statement that it is not licensed to provide clinical mental health treatment.

Two former students have filed federal lawsuits alleging they were forced to work without pay; the claims have not been decided.

Resolution Ranch

(Cameron, Texas): Texas licensing cited it in 2022 under the child's right to be "free of abuse, neglect, and exploitation" after "two children in care had inappropriate contact," and in 2024 after children reported seeing the same staff member fall asleep on night shift and after "a child in care attempted suicide and licensing was not notified." The citations are in our inspection reports.

Timothy Hill Children's Ranch

(Riverhead, New York): an employee charged with the rape of a 16-year-old resident pleaded guilty in November 2024 to a misdemeanor count of endangering the welfare of a child, RiverheadLOCAL reported.

"Help" that is really a sales funnel

Search for "help for my troubled teen" and many of the results are not programs at all. They are help lines, referral services, parent "advocates" and directories, like the ones above, that look neutral and present themselves as a free guide through a hard decision. Many are paid by the programs they recommend.

WWASP and Teen Help. The World Wide Association of Specialty Programs and Schools (WWASP) was, in the words of a federal appeals court, an organization that "does not own or operate schools; rather, it markets its members' schools to parents." Its marketing arm, Teen Help, advertised to parents in magazines and online.

Forbes reported in 1999 that an attorney suing the network called its corporate structure "extremely complex and convoluted -- deliberately so." A parent who called Teen Help was talking to the sales department of the schools it recommended.

PURE, the paid "parent advocate." Sue Scheff founded Parents Universal Resource Experts (PURE) less than a year after removing her daughter from a WWASP school, offering families information about programs; the schools it recommended competed with WWASP's.

The Tenth Circuit's opinion in the case WWASP later brought against her sets out that "PURE schools pay Ms. Scheff a substantial sum whenever a child enrolls in its program based on her recommendation," and that "Ms. Scheff used various fictitious names to post negative messages about schools for at-risk teenagers on an internet forum."

In other words, a referral broker paid per enrollment, presenting as a fellow parent, posting under made-up names to steer parents away from competitors.

Scheff later co-wrote Google Bomb, a 2009 book about online reputation, with a foreword by the head of the reputation management company ReputationDefender.

What the GAO heard when it called. "While referral services purport to offer independent advice to parents about which programs would be best for their children," Rep. George Miller said as he opened a 2008 House hearing, "the truth is that at least some of the referral services operate with significant conflicts of interest." For that hearing GAO investigators posed as parents and called programs and referral services.

One referral service's website promised, "We will look at your special situation and help you select the best school for your teen with individual attention." For three different fictional children with three different problems, it recommended the same Missouri boot camp.

GAO found the service "is owned by the husband of the boot camp owner," and the callers were never told. Two other services presented themselves as separate businesses, "with separate names, Web sites, phone numbers, and magazine advertisements," but the receptionist at one answered the phone with the other's name.

One referral agent advised the fictional father on what to tell his daughter about where she was going: "tell her it's a college prep boarding school." The referral services in GAO's cases were Teen Path and Parent Help, GAO's Gregory Kutz told the committee.

The badge. A program does not need a directory to look vetted; a trade group's logo will do. NATSAP, the National Association of Therapeutic Schools and Programs, has a logo that resembles a gold badge, and programs put it on their websites.

At a House hearing in 2007, Rep. Dale Kildee told NATSAP's executive director, Jan Moss, that he worried the credibility of membership "can be used as an advertising thing, 'We belong to NATSAP, and, obviously, we are good.'" Dr. Allison Pinto, a University of South Florida researcher, told the same hearing that when a NATSAP logo sits next to the Joint Commission seal on a website, parents tell her, "It is a NATSAP-accredited program," "even though NATSAP says, 'We are not about accreditation.'" Moss answered, "We do not want to be the Good Housekeeping seal of approval."

Six months later, one of GAO's fictional parents was told that "membership in a trade association was like a Good Housekeeping seal of approval." NATSAP did not require its members to be licensed until 2009, ten years after it was founded, as our NATSAP investigation sets out.

The trade group's own plan. NATSAP has written down how it means to be seen: members were told in 2006 to get local stories written that name their NATSAP membership and to enlist "as many of our satisfied parents and students as possible," and its strategic plans since describe an outside public relations consultant, press-release tool kits and testimonial drives. Our article In Its Own Words sets out the plans.

Educational consultants. Families who can afford it are often told to hire an "educational consultant" to choose a program for them. Many consultants are NATSAP members themselves, and our NATSAP investigation describes how they press frightened parents to decide quickly, funneling them toward member programs.

NATSAP builds that relationship on purpose: its 2019 plan describes "Link 'n Learn," "an innovative service that allows educational consultants and NATSAP programs to meet and network," and gives the consultants' trade group, IECA, an ex officio seat on NATSAP's board.

In 2021 Oregon banned residential programs from paying consultants for referrals. The industry fought the bill, arguing that without the consultants the programs would go out of business, the Associated Press reported in 2026. Oregon state Sen. Sara Gelser Blouin, who has led the state's investigations into out-of-state placements, described the arrangement this way: "you go to the consultant and $10,000 later, they make a recommendation ... It's this whole big racket." (Some program operators deny paying consultants. Ask any consultant, in writing, whether they or their firm receive anything from the programs they recommend.)

Adult addiction treatment had the same problem, and regulators acted. After The Verge exposed rehab "help lines" and review sites that were secretly marketing operations, Google restricted rehab advertising in 2017 and began requiring treatment advertisers to be certified by LegitScript.

In 2022 the Federal Trade Commission brought its first case under a new law against a rehab referral service, R360, which claimed to match people to centers "selected through a rigorous evaluation process conducted by an expert." No equivalent rule exists for programs that take children. We could find no Google advertising policy, certification or federal law that applies specifically to troubled teen referral sites.

What to do instead

  • Search every name. Find out what the program used to be called, who owns it and what else the owner runs. Our program index and network map list past names and parent companies.
  • Check where a page really is. A page named for your town or state is often written by a program hundreds of miles away, or by a directory paid to send you there. Look for the program's street address before anything else.
  • Ask who pays the person helping you. A free help line, directory or "parent advocate" is paid by someone. Ask in writing whether they receive anything from any program, and how much.
  • Read the state's reports, not the seals. Membership in a trade group is not an inspection. Look up the program's licensing and inspection history, starting at our Inspection Reports.
  • Believe the survivors over the brochure. Look for what former residents say on their own sites and forums. A program that calls its former students liars is telling you something.
  • Ask for the numbers. Ask for the average and longest length of stay in writing, and compare them with what the website says.
