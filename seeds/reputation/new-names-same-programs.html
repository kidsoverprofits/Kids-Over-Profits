The federal lawsuit filed in Indiana in April 2026 names the defendant this way: "Indiana Teen Challenge, Inc. formerly d/b/a Central Indiana Teen Challenge, now d/b/a The Refuge Girls Academy." Thirty-five women say that as girls at the home in Lebanon, between 2011 and 2024, they were put to work without pay, shut in a "safe room" for up to 30 days and, in one case, forbidden to speak for nine months. The program denies it. By the time they sued, the place they were describing had a different name.

Renaming is the troubled teen industry's oldest way out of its own record, and it is not a thing of the past. Since 2023 an Ohio program changed its sign weeks after the state stopped its admissions. A North Carolina facility the state shut down reopened at the same address "with only the name changed." Private equity companies have folded programs with deaths on their record into brands that never mention them. A parent who searches the new name finds a clean website. The record stays behind with a name nobody uses anymore.

What we found

  • Ohio suspended admissions at Mohican Young Star Academy in November 2025 over "a pattern of serious noncompliance." By December the sign on the campus read Empowering to Elevate Academy.
  • North Carolina shut Grace House in 2023 after a child's skull was allegedly fractured there. Less than a year later the company opened Silver Linings "at the same address with only the name changed."
  • Montana Academy, where 16-year-old Ben Jackson hanged himself six weeks after he arrived, reopened as Embark at Flathead Valley. Embark's website never mentions Montana Academy.
  • Sequel's founder formed a new company in 2021 to "buy back" Sequel's facilities, and kept Sequel's managers. One Alabama campus has had three names since 2022; nine girls escaped from it in a single weekend this October.
  • A federal judge dismissed trafficking claims against Triangle Cross Ranch LLC because "the current iteration did not yet exist" when the boys were there.
  • Indiana never licensed The Refuge Girls Academy. The head of its child welfare agency filmed a video at his desk calling it "a safe, faith-based residential program."

Part of our series How the Troubled Teen Industry Manages Its Reputation. Every example comes from a court record, a government or corporate filing, the company's own words or published reporting, and each is linked in the sources at the end. Where something is only alleged, or comes from a survivor or advocacy group, we say so. Our Corporatization of Residential Treatment timeline has the wider story of who bought whom.

A new sign, weeks after the state stepped in

On November 20, 2025, Ohio suspended new admissions at Mohican Young Star Academy in Perrysville, saying it "has demonstrated a pattern of serious noncompliance and/or committed violations." The Marshall Project had reported violence, injured staff and fights there after an ownership change the year before. The state had tried to close Mohican before and failed twice: the attorney general's 2021 lawsuit was dismissed, and a 2021-22 attempt to revoke its license fell through. This time Mohican, as the Marshall Project put it, "late last year rebranded as Empowering to Elevate Academy." The state now accuses it of failing to protect residents from abuse and neglect, restraining them improperly and not reporting critical incidents between April and December 2025, and is trying to revoke its license. One of its owners, Zach Logan, said they "look forward to vindicating our rights through the appropriate legal process." The academy has sued the state for $750,000.

North Carolina regulators shut down Grace House Treatment Center in Raeford in 2023, after a police child-abuse investigation in which an employee allegedly fractured a child's skull; a lawsuit says the employee had a felony record. Less than a year later, NC Health News reported, the same company, Surgeon & Associates, opened Silver Linings Treatment Center "at the same address with only the name changed." The state's own records list both names on a single page. The company says it "takes allegations of abuse seriously." (See Grace House.)

In Arkansas the new name arrived in the middle of a newspaper investigation. Piney Ridge Treatment Center, a 102-bed youth psychiatric facility in Fayetteville owned by Acadia Healthcare, had a record of sexual misconduct and other violations going back to 2019. In August 2024 a staff member was arrested, accused of showing a boy resident nude images. The Arkansas Times was running a series on the facility when, at the end of October, the state approved its new name: Yellow Rock Behavioral Health. Acadia's explanation: "There is a Piney Ridge in Missouri and a lot of times we get their publicity and vice versa." The publicity in the Arkansas Times was about Fayetteville.

Same campus, same managers, a new company

Sequel became Vivant, Brighter Path and Pathway

The U.S. Senate Finance Committee found that in 2021, "after being plagued by reporting on abuse and neglect allegations, Sequel sold 13 facilities to a newly-incorporated company, Vivant," which was "also founded by Jay Ripley" and "retained many members of Sequel leadership." Vivant's leadership told Senate investigators "there was no public facing presence for the company." Former residents suing Sequel in Alabama put it more plainly. Ripley, their complaint says, formed Vivant "to buy back the facilities the Sequel Venture was operating," and "much of Sequel's corporate management team were retained to operate the same businesses in these facilities." In October 2025 a federal judge refused to dismiss Ripley from the case.

In Alabama, the Equal Justice Initiative found, Sequel's facilities "remain open today, and the only clear change is their new name: Brighter Path." At Owens Cross Roads, where disability advocates had reported "unsafe living conditions, staff abuse and neglect, and improper and excessive use of restraints," the campus closed on April 30, 2022 for "program restructuring and transitioning of service lines" and reopened at the same location as Brighter Path. "There's a rash of bad publicity as a result of cases of abuse and neglect and then they attempt to rebrand, thinking people will forget," Tommy James, a lawyer for former residents, told WAFF. By the time police were called to what they described as a riot in September 2024, the facility was "originally known as Sequel, then Brighter Path, and now Pathway."

The city has since sued to have Pathway of Madison County declared a public nuisance; Pathway answered that closing it would "set a bad precedent for the state," and the trial is set for May 2027. On the weekend of October 10, 2026, nine girls escaped from it in three separate incidents. Police allege that a former employee drove two of them out of the county.

Sequel's Florida contracts ran on through the rebrand. In October 2022 the Department of Juvenile Justice removed every child from St. Johns Youth Academy in St. Augustine, once Three Springs of St. Johns, after an 18-year-old was accused of attacking a staff member amid allegations that staff allowed fights and had sexual relations with the youths. The department called what happened there "reprehensible, unacceptable," with "no place in Florida," and ended Sequel's contract. The campus is open again as St. Augustine Youth Academy, run by another contractor. The state's page for it does not mention its old name.

In Ohio, Sequel Pomegranate's psychiatric hospital stayed open under a settlement after state reviewers watched video of staff "striking the child's face five times with a fist, grabbing the child's hair, and pushing the child's head against the concrete block wall." In 2021 it "re-branded itself as Torii Behavioral Health," and that September it told the state it would close, in a letter from "Sequel Pomegranate Health Systems, LLC d/b/a Torii Behavioral Health." (See Sequel Pomegranate.)

Sovereign Health became Adeona

Sovereign Health Group opened a treatment center for 12- to 17-year-olds in El Cajon, California in 2014. In June 2017 the FBI searched Sovereign's offices and treatment centers in a fraud investigation. In March 2018 Brandon Nelson, 26, died by suicide a day after he was admitted to a Sovereign home in San Clemente; California's Department of Social Services found it was "a community care facility operating without a license," and his family later settled for $11 million, with no admission of liability. By January 2019 the El Cajon teen program was "licensed through a company called 'Adeona Healthcare,'" 10News reported. Adeona's president told the station that Sovereign's unpaid former staff would be paid "once the lawsuit is settled." In May 2025 Sovereign's founder, Tonmoy Sharma, was arrested on a federal indictment alleging more than $149 million in fraudulent insurance claims from 2014 to 2020; he is presumed innocent. (See Adeona Healthcare.)

Private equity: the brand replaces the name

Montana Academy became Embark

On February 12, 2017, about six weeks after his father dropped him off at Montana Academy, 16-year-old Ben Jackson "hanged himself in the bathroom," the Missoulian reported. The program's written policy treated a "Suicide attempt" as a "Capital Offense": "NO phone calls, NO mail in or out, and social isolation." It told staff to give students "a serious comeuppance, which should produce anxiety and a major disruption in the life of the student." The co-founder, John McKinnon, wrote to parents afterward: "We would be kidding ourselves if we thought we could make an open ranch completely safe for a suicidal teenager." (Our Investigatory Spotlight on John Santa covers the academy and its founders.)

In 2021 Embark Behavioral Health took over the campus, and it reopened as Embark at Flathead Valley. Its executive, clinical, program and operations directors stayed in the same jobs, according to the r/troubledteens wiki, citing their own profiles. A search of Embark's website for "Montana Academy" finds nothing. Embark's own staff page for the executive director, David Chiarito, says he "has been with Embark since the Spring of 2016." Embark did not exist until October 2018. Spring 2016, by the same wiki, is when he started at Montana Academy.

Shelterwood became Embark in a day

On January 9, 2023, Missouri's Children's Division issued two memos for the same address in Independence. One said "Doulos Ministries dba Shelterwood Academy, residential treatment license has voluntarily closed." The other said Change Academy Lake of the Ozarks, Embark's Missouri company, "has added an operating site, Embark Behavioral Health." MinistryWatch reported in 2024 that Embark, "formerly Shelterwood Academy," faces a lawsuit accusing an employee, Demarquice Phillips, of sexual assault, in which the victim's parents claim the school "continued to allow Phillips to mentor troubled girls despite allegations of abuse from several parties." Phillips is on Missouri's sex offender registry for "Sexual Contact W/ Student," an offense in Independence in 2021 against a 17-year-old girl. Today the name Shelterwood appears on Embark's site only in the filename of a photograph. (See Embark Behavioral Health, Independence.)

"We don't do roll-ups because of the negative impact it can have on quality and culture," Embark's chief executive, Alex Stavros, told an industry publication in 2023, when Consonance Capital Partners bought a majority stake for a reported $400 million. The Associated Press reported in May 2026 that over 13 years he had merged treatment centers into a chain he describes as 38 programs in 20 states, with revenue up forty-fold to $180 million. Embark's 2020 sales handbook, the AP found, says: "The admissions person sells hope when the family is at their lowest and most hopeless, scary, and vulnerable time."

Altior Healthcare, built by the Miami investment firm Pine Tree Equity, did the same with two New England programs, turning Shortridge Academy and Ironwood into "The Ridge" in 2023 and 2024. The Ridge's website names neither. Since the rename, New Hampshire inspectors have found The Ridge New Hampshire for leaving a resident unsupervised for two to three hours and, in 2025, for staff giving residents a vape pen.

Family Help & Wellness, the company behind Trails Carolina

Twelve-year-old Clark Harman died at Trails Carolina in February 2024, zipped overnight into a bivy sleeping bag used as a restraint. The medical examiner ruled his death a homicide. North Carolina revoked the program's license that October. Around it, two other programs run by its owner, Family Help & Wellness, changed their names.

The company's young adult program, Trails Momentum, was not licensed; in February 2025 the state opened an investigation into whether it should be. By December it was announcing itself as "Grow at Momentum," "previously known as Trails Momentum." (See Momentum.) Its girls' program in Weaverville had been Solstice East, then Magnolia Mill School by 2024, then Asheville Academy by 2025, and "all mention of both Solstice and Magnolia Mill has been erased from the Asheville Academy website," the Asheville Watchdog found. On May 3, 2025 a 13-year-old died by suicide there. On May 29 a 12-year-old did. The school gave up its license on June 4. (See Asheville Academy.)

The company had done this before. In 2014 it took over Island View and Aspen Institute in Syracuse, Utah, which former students said had subjected them to "cruel punishments and aggressive restraints," and reopened them as Elevations RTC and ViewPoint Center "in an effort to make a clean break from our previous corporate owner." In July 2026 Elevations, ViewPoint and three other Utah programs announced that they had joined "EOSIS Utah." EOSIS is itself a new name: until February 2025 it was Meridian Behavioral Health, which called the change "a new beginning, with new leadership." None of the announcements says who sold the programs. (See our Elevations RTC profile and ViewPoint Center.)

No license to lose

Religious programs that answer to no state licensing agency rename just as easily, and nobody has to approve it.

A spokesperson for Indiana's Department of Child Services confirmed that The Refuge Girls Academy "was not licensed by DCS" because licensing was "not required because of its religious affiliation." In November 2025, months before the lawsuit, the department's director, Adam Krupp, posted a video on the Refuge's Facebook page, filmed at his DCS desk with the DCS logo, calling it "a safe, faith-based residential program." In April 2026 its voicemail still greeted callers as Central Indiana Teen Challenge. Its lawyer called the women's complaint "deliberately outrageous," and in court it argued that residents "knowingly, voluntarily and freely consented to participate in the Teen Challenge rehabilitation program," and that silencing a girl was a "behavioral intervention" for "impulse control." It closed on September 10, 2026, saying the cost of the lawsuit made it "no longer tenable." The lawsuit goes on.

In 2012 the Tampa Bay Times named Anderson Academy in Vero Beach, Florida among seven unlicensed religious homes that accounted for two-thirds of a decade's abuse hotline complaints. Renamed Treasure Coast Boys Academy and owned by Teen Challenge of Florida, it was where a staff member of at least five years was arrested in March 2024, accused of molesting a student. A family's lawsuit says the school had warnings and "put Nicholas Wagner back in the school." The campus has since closed.

In Wyoming, Mount Carmel Youth Ranch turned in its license in 2012 and kept boarding boys as Triangle Cross Ranch, whose owner told the state he had moved them across the line into Montana "to avoid Wyoming certification and regulatory oversight." When its license ended in 2023 after 80 rule violations in nine years, it became Sunlight Mountain Boys Ranch, unlicensed, with a website NBC News found "nearly identical to the Triangle Cross Ranch website" that "does not mention its previous name." Two former residents sued over forced labor. In 2024 the judge dismissed the claims against Triangle Cross Ranch LLC because, Cowboy State Daily reported, "the current iteration did not yet exist" when they were there. Sunlight Mountain shut down by the end of 2024. Its sister program for girls, Trinity Teen Solutions, settled a class action by 262 women for $2.3 million, with no admission of wrongdoing. (See Triangle Cross Ranch.)

The oldest trick

None of this is new. In 1992 "Straight-Orlando shut its doors on August 14," Orlando Weekly reported. "But it re-opened for business the same day, in the same facility with the same administration. The only real difference was the name: SAFE." In 2007 the Government Accountability Office told Congress that five of the nine programs it investigated were "a permutation of the original program operating under a different name or in a new location." At the same hearing Paul Lewis, whose son Ryan died at Alldredge Academy in West Virginia seven days after he arrived, described the man who ran it.

Alldredge, by then owned by Solacium, became Alldredge Wilderness Journey, and Solacium became InnerChange, one of the companies Embark was formed from. After its 2009 bankruptcy, Hidden Lake Academy in Georgia told parents why it no longer existed: "Our attorneys have advised us to 'rebrand'. Therefore, HLA ceased to exist and a separate corporation Ridge Creek School was created." And Arizona Boys Ranch, where 16-year-old Nicholaus Contreraz died in 1998, became Canyon State Academy "to help improve its image after a student died amid allegations of abuse." It still operates under that name. Between January 2024 and December 2025, police took 208 reports of boys missing from it.

A rename only works if someone lets it

Every rename above worked because a regulator, a court, a referral list or a parent treated the new name as a new program. Ohio's effort to close Mohican has now outlasted two of its names. North Carolina's own records hold Grace House and Silver Linings on one page, and Silver Linings opened anyway. A federal judge let a ranch company out of a trafficking suit because it was formed after the boys left. Indiana's child welfare director vouched for a home his department had never licensed.

It does not have to work. When Diamond Ranch Academy lost its Utah license in 2023 for "severe physical neglect" and an application was filed to reopen it as RAFA Academy with "some of the same employees," the state never licensed it, and the city of Hurricane revoked its business license. When Utah revoked both Provo Canyon School licenses in July 2026, it barred the owners from applying again for five years; Universal Health Services is appealing. A rule that follows the owners, the managers and the address catches a rename. A rule that follows the name never will. A bill now in the Senate would have states check sister facilities "that share ownership" within 30 days of a substantiated serious complaint. Nothing in it follows a program to its next name.

Every past name we know of is on the program's page on this site, and the search finds a program by any of them.
