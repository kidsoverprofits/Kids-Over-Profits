In 2021, after five states had pulled their children out of Sequel Youth and Family Services and a 16-year-old had died under its staff's weight in Michigan, Sequel's co-founder Jay Ripley formed a new company and bought 13 of Sequel's facilities. The U.S. Senate Finance Committee spelled out what that meant: "Ripley ultimately repurchased several RTFs his company had sold to private equity four years earlier." The children stayed in the same buildings. Much of the management stayed in the same jobs. The company had a new name, and so, one by one, did the programs.

The troubled teen industry's most important renames are not of programs but of companies. When a chain's programs get too hot, it closes or sells them, and the same people come back under a new company name to run the same campuses under new program names. Aspen Education Group shed its programs, and the people who left Aspen built Family Help & Wellness out of the ones it closed. Sequel became Vivant. Embark Behavioral Health is a brand laid over the records of half a dozen older programs and companies. A parent who searches the name on the sign today finds none of it.

What we found

  • Sequel's founder formed a new company in 2021 and bought back 13 Sequel facilities. The Senate found the two companies "share leadership and staff, raising questions about whether Vivant is a new, wholly distinct entity." Vivant then sold off nine of them while the Senate was investigating.
  • Aspen Education Group closed program after program between 2009 and 2014, after a boy's death at Youth Care, a heat-stroke death at SageWalk and nine confirmed abuse findings at Mount Bachelor. Aspen's former chief financial officer had already founded Family Help & Wellness, which reopened three of Aspen's closed campuses under new names.
  • Family Help & Wellness's own programs then piled up deaths: Alec Lansing in 2014, Clark Harman in 2024, two girls, 13 and 12, in 2025. In June 2026 it sold its Utah programs, the old Aspen campuses among them, to a Minnesota company that runs them as "EOSIS Utah."
  • What was left of Aspen went to Acadia Healthcare, where the Senate found staff at Piney Ridge had done 110 restraints and seclusions in 30 days. In 2024 Piney Ridge got a new name.
  • Embark's chief executive told an industry publication in 2023, "We don't do roll-ups." Embark is a 2018 umbrella over four companies that has since taken over the Montana Academy and Shelterwood campuses, and its website mentions neither.

Sequel bought itself back

Jay Ripley learned the business at Youth Services International, the juvenile corrections company he started with the founder of Jiffy Lube. In 1999 he, Adam Shapiro and Cindy Cox founded Sequel to take over one of YSI's old programs. Sequel grew by buying other companies' programs and renaming them: Three Springs, Inc. of Alabama at the end of 2009, whose programs became "Sequel TSI," then Camelot, then YSI's own Florida contracts, then Pomegranate in Ohio. By 2017 it ran 44 programs in 19 states and took in $208 million a year, almost all of it public money. Ripley explained the model to business students in 2015: "You can make money in this business if you control staffing." (See Sequel and Three Springs.)

The record followed. On April 29, 2020, staff at Sequel's Lakeside Academy in Kalamazoo restrained 16-year-old Cornelius Frederick for throwing a sandwich; Michigan found "several were observed on the video with their weight on Resident A's chest, abdomen, and legs." He died on May 1. APM Reports found 20 cases since 2010 in which government investigators concluded that Sequel staff had sexually abused or harassed children. Oregon, Minnesota, Maryland, Washington and California brought their children home. Sequel closed half its residential centers.

Then Ripley formed Vivant Behavioral Healthcare, with his daughter, a former Sequel vice president, signing its Alabama registration, and bought 13 facilities back. The Senate found Vivant "does not have any public-facing corporate presence" and that the two companies "share leadership and staff, raising questions about whether Vivant is a new, wholly distinct entity." Former residents suing in Alabama allege Vivant was formed "to buy back the facilities the Sequel Venture was operating" and that "much of Sequel's corporate management team were retained to operate the same businesses in these facilities"; in October 2025 a federal judge refused to dismiss Ripley from that case. During the Senate's investigation, Vivant sold off its nine facilities in Alabama and Florida. Oregon State Senator Sara Gelser: "It's like this many-headed Hydra. You cut off the head. You think you're making progress, and then it just comes back stronger. … The name isn't the problem. The leadership is the problem."

The programs changed names on the way through. Sequel's Alabama company became Brighter Path of Alabama, and the Equal Justice Initiative found its facilities "remain open today, and the only clear change is their new name." At Owens Cross Roads, where disability advocates had reported "staff abuse and neglect, and improper and excessive use of restraints," the campus closed on April 30, 2022 and reopened at the same location as Brighter Path. "There's a rash of bad publicity as a result of cases of abuse and neglect and then they attempt to rebrand, thinking people will forget," Tommy James, a lawyer for former residents, told WAFF. By 2024 the place was "originally known as Sequel, then Brighter Path, and now Pathway." The city has sued to have Pathway of Madison County declared a public nuisance, with trial set for May 2027. On the weekend of October 10, 2026, nine girls escaped from it.

At Brighter Path Tuskegee, a child died after self-inflicted injuries while Vivant owned it, and a 2024 lawsuit alleges staff instigated fights between residents and gambled on them. Alabama removed all 28 boys from Brighter Path Courtland in 2024 after fire officials found emergency exits blocked. In Florida the Department of Juvenile Justice ended Vivant's contract for St. Johns Youth Academy in 2022, citing "the contracted provider's complete failure to ensure a safe environment," and the campus reopened under another contractor as St. Augustine Youth Academy. In Ohio, Sequel Pomegranate, where state reviewers watched video of staff "striking the child's face five times with a fist," had become "Torii Behavioral Health" by the time it closed in 2021. (See Vivant.)

Aspen's closed programs, reopened by Aspen's people

Aspen Education Group was assembled by private equity firms from 1998 and by 2006 ran 32 programs in 12 states: wilderness programs, boarding schools and residential treatment centers. That year CRC Health, newly bought by Bain Capital, acquired Aspen for $296.4 million.

Its record grew with it. On June 28, 2007, 14-year-old Brendan Blum died at Aspen's Youth Care of Utah of a twisted bowel; staff had not called the on-call nurse while he was vomiting, and two counselors were charged. In November 2009 Oregon suspended the license of Mount Bachelor Academy after confirming nine allegations of child abuse and neglect, including sexualized role-playing in front of other students, sleep deprivation and long manual labor as punishment; 51 former students later sued. The same year Oregon shut down Aspen's SageWalk wilderness program after a 16-year-old died of heat stroke on a hike.

Then Aspen began shedding programs. Cedars Academy closed in 2009. Aspen Ranch, Bromley Brook, New Leaf and SunHawk closed in March 2011. The Academy at Swift River, Talisman, Stone Mountain, SUWS and Adirondack Leadership Expeditions went in the summer of 2013. Island View in Syracuse, Utah, where former students said they were subjected to "cruel punishments and aggressive restraints," closed in April 2014.

It reopened that spring in the same building as Elevations RTC. The company that reopened it, Family Help & Wellness, had been founded in 2008 by Tim Dupell, Aspen's former executive vice president and chief financial officer, and had taken its first private equity money, from Trinity Hunt Partners, in 2014. It reopened Aspen Institute next door as ViewPoint Center and Aspen's Copper Canyon Academy in Arizona as Sedona Sky Academy. "In an effort to make a clean break from our previous corporate owner," the new owner wrote, "we have opened two new programs under new ownership." Police calls to the Syracuse campus rose about 40 percent after it reopened. (See our Elevations RTC profile.)

What was left of Aspen went to Acadia Healthcare with CRC in 2015. The Senate later found that at Acadia's Piney Ridge Treatment Center in Fayetteville, Arkansas, "staff routinely simultaneously chemically restrained and secluded children, in violation of federal regulation," with "110 restraints and seclusions in a 30-day period." When Senate staff asked whether that was a problem, "Acadia executives objected to the premise of the question." In August 2024 a Piney Ridge staff member was arrested, accused of showing a boy resident nude images, and the Arkansas Times was running a series on the facility when the state approved its new name that October: Yellow Rock Behavioral Health. Acadia's explanation: "There is a Piney Ridge in Missouri and a lot of times we get their publicity and vice versa." (See Acadia Healthcare.)

Then Family Help & Wellness got too hot

Family Help & Wellness grew to 20 programs by 2021, and its record grew the way Aspen's had. Alec Lansing, 17, died of hypothermia in 2014 after running away from Trails Carolina. North Carolina investigated Solstice East every year from 2018 to 2021. On February 3, 2024, 12-year-old Clark Harman died at Trails Carolina a day after he arrived, zipped overnight into a bivy sleeping bag used as a restraint. The medical examiner ruled it a homicide. North Carolina removed every child and revoked the program's license that May.

The renames came around it. The girls' program in Weaverville went from Solstice East to Magnolia Mill School by 2024 and to Asheville Academy by 2025, and "all mention of both Solstice and Magnolia Mill has been erased from the Asheville Academy website," the Asheville Watchdog found. On May 3, 2025 a 13-year-old died by suicide there. On May 29 a 12-year-old did. The school gave up its license on June 4, and the state fined the company $45,000. The unlicensed young adult program Trails Momentum was "Grow at Momentum" by that December. (See Asheville Academy and Momentum.)

On June 23, 2026 EOSIS announced that it had bought Elevations, ViewPoint and three other Utah programs from Family Help & Wellness, to run as "EOSIS Utah" under their old names. EOSIS is itself a new name: until February 2025 it was Meridian Behavioral Health, which called the change "a new beginning, with new leadership." The programs' own announcements, posted over the next month, never said who had sold them. The Syracuse building that was Aspen's Island View is now on its third company in twelve years.

Embark: one brand over everyone's past

On October 31, 2018, four companies, Calo, InnerChange, New Vision Wilderness and Potomac Pathways, announced a new "umbrella name": Embark Behavioral Health. InnerChange had been Solacium, the company that bought Alldredge Academy in West Virginia after 16-year-old Ryan Lewis died there seven days after he arrived. His father told Congress in 2007 about the man who had run it.

Solacium renamed the school Alldredge Wilderness Journey, then renamed itself InnerChange, and InnerChange became part of Embark. Under the new brand the company kept taking over other programs' campuses. On February 12, 2017, about six weeks after his father dropped him off at Montana Academy, 16-year-old Ben Jackson "hanged himself in the bathroom," the Missoulian reported. The program's written policy treated a "Suicide attempt" as a "Capital Offense": "NO phone calls, NO mail in or out, and social isolation." In 2021 Embark took over the campus, and it reopened as Embark at Flathead Valley, with Montana Academy's executive, clinical, program and operations directors in the same jobs, according to the r/troubledteens wiki. A search of Embark's website for "Montana Academy" finds nothing. Its page for the executive director says he "has been with Embark since the Spring of 2016," two years before Embark existed. (Our Investigatory Spotlight on John Santa covers Montana Academy and its founders.)

On January 9, 2023, Missouri issued two memos for one address in Independence. One said Shelterwood Academy's "residential treatment license has voluntarily closed." The other said Calo "has added an operating site, Embark Behavioral Health." MinistryWatch reported that Embark, "formerly Shelterwood Academy," faces a lawsuit by parents who claim the school "continued to allow Phillips to mentor troubled girls despite allegations of abuse from several parties"; Demarquice Phillips is on Missouri's sex offender registry for "Sexual Contact W/ Student." The name Shelterwood now appears on Embark's site only in the filename of a photograph. (See Embark Behavioral Health, Independence.)

"We don't do roll-ups because of the negative impact it can have on quality and culture," Embark's chief executive, Alex Stavros, told an industry publication in 2023, when Consonance Capital Partners bought a majority stake for a reported $400 million. The Associated Press reported in 2026 that he had merged treatment centers into a chain he describes as 38 programs in 20 states with $180 million in revenue, and that Embark's sales handbook says: "The admissions person sells hope when the family is at their lowest and most hopeless, scary, and vulnerable time." Fifteen former residents have sued Calo since 2024, and Missouri has made five findings of physical abuse and five of sexual abuse involving it; Calo denies the allegations.

One program at a time

Single programs still do it the old way.

In Ohio, the state suspended admissions at Mohican Young Star Academy on November 20, 2025 over "a pattern of serious noncompliance," after two earlier attempts to close it had failed. It had "rebranded as Empowering to Elevate Academy" by December, the Marshall Project reported. The state is now trying to revoke its license; one of its owners said they "look forward to vindicating our rights through the appropriate legal process."

In North Carolina, regulators shut down Grace House Treatment Center in Raeford in 2023 after a police child-abuse investigation in which an employee allegedly fractured a child's skull. Less than a year later the same company opened Silver Linings Treatment Center "at the same address with only the name changed," NC Health News reported. (See Grace House.)

In Indiana, the federal lawsuit 35 women filed in 2026 names the defendant "Indiana Teen Challenge, Inc. formerly d/b/a Central Indiana Teen Challenge, now d/b/a The Refuge Girls Academy." They allege forced labor, isolation in a "safe room" for up to 30 days and, for one, nine months forbidden to speak, between 2011 and 2024; the program denies it. Indiana never licensed the home because of its religious affiliation, yet in November 2025 the director of the state's Department of Child Services filmed a video at his desk calling it "a safe, faith-based residential program." The Refuge closed in September 2026, saying the lawsuit made it "no longer tenable." In Florida, Teen Challenge's Anderson Academy, named by the Tampa Bay Times in 2012 among the unlicensed religious homes behind two-thirds of a decade's abuse hotline complaints, became Treasure Coast Boys Academy, where a staff member was arrested in 2024, accused of molesting a student.

In Wyoming, Triangle Cross Ranch lost its license in 2023 after 80 rule violations in nine years and kept going, unlicensed, as Sunlight Mountain Boys Ranch, with a website NBC News found "nearly identical" that "does not mention its previous name." When two former residents sued over forced labor, a judge dismissed the claims against Triangle Cross Ranch LLC because, Cowboy State Daily reported, "the current iteration did not yet exist" when they were there. (See Triangle Cross Ranch.)

The name is the only thing that changes

"Large for-profit companies are able to evade accountability by closing down facilities when investigations begin to interfere with administration or operation," the National Disability Rights Network wrote in 2021. "Despite this fact, they continue to buy out and open up additional facilities around the country." The Senate Finance Committee made it a key finding in 2024: "Exploiting corporate structures can enable RTF operators to evade oversight."

States license a program, not the people who run it, and a license dies with a name. Every company above has used that. Utah has shown it does not have to work: when Diamond Ranch Academy lost its license in 2023 and an application was filed to reopen it as RAFA Academy with "some of the same employees," the state never licensed it, and when Utah revoked both Provo Canyon School licenses in July 2026 it barred the owners from applying again for five years. A bill now in the Senate would have states check sister facilities "that share ownership" within 30 days of a substantiated serious complaint. Ownership is exactly what changes hands when Sequel becomes Vivant, or when Aspen's closed programs become Family Help & Wellness's.
