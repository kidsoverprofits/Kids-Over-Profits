Introduction

Provo Canyon School is a locked residential treatment program for teenagers in Utah County, Utah, founded in 1971 and owned since 2000 by Universal Health Services, the largest for-profit behavioral health operator in the United States. It has marketed itself to families, insurers, and state juvenile justice and child welfare systems as a residential treatment center for youth aged roughly 11 to 17, with tuition reported at more than $12,500 a month and an average stay of eight to twelve months.

For most of its five decades the program has faced allegations of abuse: a 1974 closure over child abuse and neglect allegations, a federal injunction in 1980 limiting its use of isolation, restraint, polygraphs and mail censorship, a string of civil suits through the 1980s and 1990s, and since 2020 a sustained public reckoning led by survivors including Paris Hilton. In July 2026 the Utah Department of Health and Human Services revoked the licenses of both campuses, citing repeated failures to keep children safe, and ordered the program to cease operations. Provo Canyon School appealed on July 27, 2026. As of September 2026 its operations are suspended while that appeal is pending; the school is not closed, and survivors have publicly urged the state not to allow it to reopen.

Provo Canyon School is also a hub of the wider troubled teen industry. Founders and staff who passed through it went on to create or run Logan River Academy, Discovery Academy, Cinnamon Hills, Silverado Academy, Copper Hills Youth Center and, most consequentially, the World Wide Association of Specialty Programs and Schools, whose founder Robert Lichfield began his career there as a dorm parent in 1977.

History

  • 1971: Provo Canyon School opens in Provo as an all-boys facility, founded by physician Robert H. Crist and Jack Williams. Its early program included physical restraint, physical punishment, isolation from the outside world, polygraph testing, monitoring of personal communication and administration of drugs.
  • 1974: The school is shut down over allegations of child abuse and neglect. Crist and Williams reopen it later the same year.
  • 1977: Robert B. Lichfield begins work at the school as a dorm parent. He later founds Cross Creek and the World Wide Association of Specialty Programs and Schools (WWASP).
  • 1978 to 1986: Dennis Eugene Thorne is co-owner. In 1989 he founds Discovery Academy with fellow Provo Canyon staff Robert Crist and Brent Hall.
  • Late 1970s: Two boys placed by their home states' juvenile justice systems, Timothy Milonas of Nevada and Kenneth Rice of Alaska, escape and file a federal lawsuit against the school and its owners challenging its education, treatment and confinement methods.
  • 1980: After a lengthy trial a jury sides with the school, but the judge issues a permanent injunction barring it from opening or reading boys' mail, administering polygraphs, placing boys in isolation for any reason other than to contain physical violence, and using physical force except to restrain a boy who is an immediate danger to himself or others.
  • 1986: Charter Behavioral Health Systems, then the largest owner of inpatient psychiatric facilities in the country, buys the school and owns it for fourteen years.
  • 1999: A CBS 60 Minutes II investigation, "Unsafe Haven," documents unqualified staff, falsified records, dangerous restraint practices and insurance fraud across Charter's hospitals. Paris Hilton spends eleven months at the Provo Canyon girls campus in Orem this year.
  • 2000: Charter files for bankruptcy. On August 21, 2000, Universal Health Services acquires eleven Charter facilities, Provo Canyon School among them, for $105 million. Barry Woodward is appointed chief executive. Co-founder Robert Crist and therapist Larry Carter leave to found Logan River Academy.
  • 2005: A new girls campus opens in Springville. The school joins NATSAP the same year. The former Orem girls campus becomes Provo Canyon Behavioral Hospital.
  • 2009: The school files a libel suit against the human rights group HEAL, which had been publishing records of police calls and complaints about the program. HEAL describes the suit as a strategic lawsuit against public participation.
  • 2012: Utah regulators place the Provo campus on conditional status after staff fail to supervise students and a boy runs away, steals a car and causes a fatal crash.
  • 2013: The Springville campus is placed on conditional status after a girl is injured during a restraint.
  • 2015 to 2020: The Utah Office of Licensing conducts 341 investigations across the school's campuses and substantiates 27 violations.
  • 2017: Parents of a former resident sue for negligence, alleging their daughter was denied care for a broken thumb for nearly two weeks and given access to objects she used to harm herself.
  • 2020: Paris Hilton's documentary This Is Paris (September 14) names the school and describes being drugged, restrained and held in solitary confinement. The Salt Lake Tribune reports that six women who attended between 2003 and 2017 describe similar overmedication, restraint and punitive practices under UHS ownership. Survivors organize the first large protest at the school on October 9. The school responds that UHS bought the program after Hilton left and is not responsible for earlier events.
  • 2021: Paris Hilton testifies before the Utah Senate Judiciary Committee in February in support of new oversight legislation. In August, former resident Aaron Ross files a federal suit against UHS and the school alleging sexual abuse by a staff member, chemical sedation, isolation and food and sleep deprivation during his stay in 2001. APM Reports publishes "The teen got a concussion. The school got a pass."
  • June 26, 2024: Paris Hilton testifies before the House Ways and Means Committee about abuse at the school and other programs. The school declines to comment and refers to a 2021 statement about its 2000 change of ownership.
  • September 2024: Staff member Mulimauga Tuiloma is arrested after striking a 15-year-old student during what he described as a restraint. He pleads guilty in July 2025 and receives a suspended sentence with probation.
  • February 2025: The Salt Lake Tribune reports that Utah licensers found staff hurt children in at least seven documented incidents over two years, including video of a staff member striking a child during a restraint, and placed the Springville campus under heightened scrutiny while it continued to house more than 100 teenagers.
  • June 2026: Utah licensers sanction the school for delaying medical care to a teenager who suffered a jaw injury and brain bleed in a fight at the facility, barring new admissions. Two families file lawsuits alleging medical neglect. The Disability Law Center reports that teens are not safe at either campus, citing violence, self-harm, inadequate medical care and chronic staffing shortages.
  • July 17, 2026: The Utah Department of Health and Human Services revokes the Provo campus license for repeated noncompliance, citing failures to protect clients from harm and the use of cruel and unnecessary practices, and orders it to cease operations by August 16. The girls program is ordered closed by August. The school appeals both orders on July 27.
  • August 2026: YouTuber Reckless Ben releases hidden-camera footage recorded while posing as a resident, showing staff training on physical restraint. Paris Hilton and survivor Jen Robison publish commentaries urging Utah not to allow the school to reopen. Families report scrambling for alternative placements as operations are suspended. The appeal remains pending.

Ownership

Founders (1971 to 1986). Robert H. Crist served for years as the school's medical director and psychiatrist. He went on to co-found Logan River Academy in 2000 and Silverado Academy in 2006, the latter closed in 2016 following reported sexual abuse. Jack Williams later founded Cinnamon Hills Youth Crisis Center in St. George; he died in 2009.

Charter Behavioral Health Systems (1986 to 2000). Charter grew to 91 hospitals treating 120,000 inpatients a year, including 35,000 children and adolescents, before the 1999 CBS investigation into the restraint death of 16-year-old Tristan Sovern at a Charter hospital in North Carolina and a federal inspector general inquiry. Charter collapsed into bankruptcy in 2000 and sold its remaining hospitals.

Universal Health Services (2000 to present). UHS, headquartered in King of Prussia, Pennsylvania, acquired Provo Canyon School in a $105 million purchase of eleven Charter facilities on August 21, 2000. UHS also bought and continued to operate several former CEDU schools after that chain collapsed in 2005. UHS has said it is not responsible for conduct at the school before its purchase; survivors who attended after 2000 describe the same practices, and several long-tenured staff remained for years after the sale.

Notable staff and where they went. Robert B. Lichfield, dorm parent from 1977, founded Cross Creek and WWASP. Karr Farnsworth, a colleague of Lichfield's at the school, later served as WWASP's president. Kreg Gillman, chief executive from 2003 to 2006, helped found Silverado Academy, was clinical director of Redcliff Ascent, and became chief executive of UHS Salt Lake Behavioral Health. Adam McClain, group director and chief executive, was previously clinical director of Peninsula Village and executive director of Northwest Academy, a WWASP program. Ron Tuinei, program and executive director from 2016 to 2020, came from Heritage RTC, Cottonwood Treatment Center and the Oakley School and became chief executive of Copper Hills Youth Center. Barry Woodward, chief executive from 2000, also later ran Copper Hills. Jennifer Morgan Smith, at the school since 1998 in clinical and admissions roles, became executive director of Provo Canyon Behavioral Hospital.

Program and Practices

The school describes an "Acuity Based Care" model. Survivors and former staff describe a behavior modification program built on a level system, points-based punishment, seclusion and physical restraint. On arrival residents were historically strip-searched and assigned a number used in place of their name. Communication with family was restricted and monitored; letters were read and phone calls ended if a resident spoke negatively about the program.

Levels. Eight levels are reported, from Orientation (no privileges, about two weeks) through Pre-team, Team (first family contact), Advanced Team (off-campus excursions), Achievement, Advanced Achievement, Senior and Advanced Senior. Level drops for minor infractions could reset a resident's expected release date.

Investment. Residents accumulated "investment points" for infractions as minor as talking back or being late in line. Points were "served off" in the Investment Unit sitting motionless in "chair structure," feet flat, for hours or days, in sweatpants and without personal items. Residents describe missing school for days at a time while serving points.

Observation. "Obs" was the school's term for seclusion: bare concrete rooms about five feet square with a floor drain, a small window and cameras. Survivors describe stays from hours to days, sometimes weeks, often without clothing or bedding, in rooms kept deliberately cold. A 2004 complaint by a parent and registered nurse described a 15-year-old whose nose was broken before a forced injection of Haldol, and children held in observation most of the day.

Dial 9. Staff used the radio code "Dial 9" to summon a restraint team. Other residents were made to face the wall. Survivors and a former staff member describe residents tackled, dragged to seclusion and injected with sedatives, a practice residents called "booty juice," with injuries including bruising, abrasions and broken noses. The 1980 injunction had limited restraint to cases of immediate danger; Utah licensing findings in 2024 and 2025 document staff striking, pushing and injuring residents during restraints.

Medication. Survivors across three decades describe being given multiple psychotropic medications without clear diagnosis, sedated for emotional distress, and punished with seclusion for refusing medication. One former resident describes being prescribed up to seven medications at once by Crist, including an antipsychotic, without a corresponding diagnosis.

Utah Licensing Record

Kids Over Profits mirrors Utah Office of Licensing inspection records for both campuses on the Utah inspection reports page. Between December 2022 and August 2025 the Springville campus (license 93697) was inspected 64 times, including 29 investigation inspections and 10 conditional monitoring visits, and 7 rule violations were substantiated. The Provo campus (license 93696) was inspected 18 times in the same period with no substantiated findings in the mirrored records. Both licenses were on conditional status with an expiration date of January 31, 2027 when they were revoked. The revocations are under appeal and operations are suspended in the meantime.

Substantiated findings at the Springville campus:

  • April 22, 2024: Staffing ratio. Two staff were supervising 14 youth against a required ratio of one to five (R501-19-4).
  • September 12, 2024: Client rights. Video of a critical incident showed a staff member acting in a way that harmed clients (R380-80-6).
  • November 26, 2024: Client rights. Video showed staff initiating a restraint that did not fall under the Handle With Care training (R380-80-6).
  • December 3, 2024: Client rights, repeat noncompliance. Video from December 1 showed a staff member striking a client during a restraint (R380-80-6).
  • March 10, 2025: Protection of clients. Video showed a staff member physically pushing a client (R380-80-4).
  • April 23, 2025: Client rights and safe practices, two findings. During a restraint on April 13 a staff member injured a client's face and did not follow the Handle With Care policy on which they had been trained (R380-80-6, R380-80-5).

Inspection activity intensified in 2026. State records list a complaint investigation at the Provo campus on June 19, 2026 with 16 findings, a complaint investigation at Springville on June 2, 2026 with 22 findings and a focus inspection on June 23 with 3 more, followed by conditional monitoring and follow-up visits through early July. The revocations followed on July 17.

Lawsuits

  • Milonas and Rice v. Provo Canyon School (U.S. District Court, Utah, decided 1980). Two boys placed by Nevada and Alaska challenged the school's confinement and treatment practices. The jury found for the school; the court entered a permanent injunction against polygraphs, mail censorship and the use of isolation and force except to contain immediate physical danger, finding the practices anti-therapeutic and a denial of due process. The ruling and memorandum are in the document library below.
  • Taylor v. Provo Canyon School and Charter Medical (Tenth Circuit). One of several suits through the 1980s and 1990s alleging false imprisonment, medical negligence, fraud and breach of fiduciary duty. HEAL maintains a compiled list of cases against the school.
  • Provo Canyon School v. HEAL (filed December 2009). The school sued the human rights group for libel over its published records of police calls and complaints.
  • Doe v. Provo Canyon School and Island View RTC (U.S. District Court, Utah, 2014 to 2015). Negligence and breach of fiduciary duty claims naming therapist Wade Taylor among the defendants.
  • Chudnovsky v. Provo Canyon School (filed January 3, 2017). Negligence and intentional infliction of emotional distress over a broken thumb left untreated for nearly two weeks and access to self-harm implements.
  • Ross v. Universal Health Services, Inc. et al. (U.S. District Court, Utah, filed August 2021). A former resident, aged 13 in 2001, alleges repeated sexual abuse by a staff member, chemical sedation, extended isolation, strip searches and two years without going outdoors.
  • Two family suits (June 2026). Families allege medical neglect and malpractice, including a teenager who was warned he was unsafe before an attack that left him with a jaw injury and brain bleed, and delayed medical care. Paris Hilton publicly supported both families.
  • Lichfield v. Netflix (Tenth Circuit, 2026). Not a suit against the school, but the court rejected WWASP founder Narvin Lichfield's defamation claim over the documentary series The Program, which features allegations about Provo Canyon School.

Survivor Voices

Content warning: the accounts linked here describe restraint, seclusion, forced medication, sexual abuse and suicide.

Paris Hilton spent eleven months at the Orem girls campus in 1999 after running from three CEDU programs. In This Is Paris (2020) and in testimony to the Utah Senate (2021) and the U.S. House Ways and Means Committee (2024) she has described being referred to by a number, strip-searched, forced to sit motionless in a chair for days, medicated into numbness, and held naked in a cold concrete seclusion room for 24 hours after faking a dose. She describes the school as "the worst of the worst" of the programs she was sent to. Her full account is published by Survivors Breaking Silence, and her August 2026 commentary in The Salt Lake Tribune urges Utah not to let the school reopen.

Jen Robison, a survivor featured in the Netflix series The Program, wrote in The Salt Lake Tribune in July 2026 that children deserve safe help and that the closure must be permanent.

Kat Von D has said she was sent for a three-week program and stayed six months, witnessed residents force-fed medication, sedated and isolated, and left with lasting trauma.

Alexa (Breaking Code Silence) was sent in September 2004, two months after her father's suicide, and describes a roommate, a childhood sexual abuse survivor, sedated to the point of falling asleep and repeatedly placed in observation for it.

Amie Ashcraft (Fornits, 2004) describes six months at the Orem campus in 1999 and 2000: journals about her rape confiscated as "inappropriate," grievances intercepted by her therapist, seven concurrent medications prescribed by Robert Crist, injections of Haldol or Thorazine during a PTSD flashback, and an escape under a plastic fence after months without fresh air. Her family was billed $6,000 a month.

Further first-person accounts, including video testimonies from 2009 and 2020, are collected in the r/troubledteens wiki entry, which Kids Over Profits mirrors, and at HEAL.
