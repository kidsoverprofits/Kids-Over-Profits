Aspen Ranch was a residential treatment center for teenagers in Loa, Utah, run by Aspen Education Group, a division of CRC Health Group, until 2011.[1][2] It combined a working horse ranch with a school.[3] A former student there in 2008 and 2009 has said staff pinned teenagers face down when they refused orders and that one staff member broke a boy's wrist.[4] Aspen Education Group closed the ranch in 2011, saying demand for therapeutic programs had fallen.[2]

Its 2009 listing with the National Association of Therapeutic Schools and Programs gave 1995 as its founding year and 1999 as the year it joined.[5] The ranch was operating by 2000.[3] That September it had 55 students aged 13 to 17, with room for 70; new arrivals lived in a building called "Roundup," and students moved up through levels named Mustang, Maverick, Greenhorn and Rancher.[3] Kreg Gillman was executive director, and the average stay was about five months.[3] Scott Hess was clinical director until 2000.[6] The Utah Department of Human Services licensed it as a residential treatment center.[3][7] In 2010 Woodbury Reports said CARF had accredited it for three years.[8]

By 2009 the ranch listed 80 students aged 13 to 18, stays of six to 12 months, and Melodie Rose as its executive contact.[5] Woodbury Reports named Brandon Burr as clinical director in 2009.[9] The ranch's enrollment agreement set the price at $350 a day with a minimum stay of nine months.[10] The first payment was $18,715: the first month's tuition of $7,982.50, a $7,982.50 prepaid fee for transition services that the program kept if the child left early, and a $2,750 enrollment fee.[10] The agreement named Robin Stephens as Aspen Education's regional director.[10] Parents handed Aspen temporary custody of the child by power of attorney, and the agreement also provided for students placed in Aspen's custody by a court.[10] It called for a strip search and a search of all belongings at intake.[10] It also allowed staff to physically restrain, control and detain students, including to bring back runaways.[10] The program details in the same packet said the first eight weeks were a time of adjustment that went better with family and student apart, so parent visits could be arranged only after the eighth week.[10] Students could receive letters from friends only in the final stages of treatment and with their parents' approval.[10] Those who reached the top levels, Rider and Wrangler, could earn off-campus trips, which the packet said usually took at least six months.[10] It said a psychiatrist visited the ranch once a month and a family nurse practitioner once a week, and that the school was accredited by the Northwest Association of Accredited Schools.[10]

Former students have described what that meant in practice.[11][12] One who was there around 2005 said new arrivals spent their first weeks on "round-up," either silent in a basement or doing manual labor outside, with no contact with their parents.[11] When the student refused to go outside one July morning, the student said, staff ripped the sheets out from under the student, threw the student onto a staircase and dragged the student to the field to work.[11] The student said all letters were read before they were sent, weekly calls home were on speakerphone with a therapist and were cut off if a student described abuse, and students were threatened with transfer to a program outside the country.[11] Another former student, writing in June 2006, said staff restrained students with the Positive Control System, using pressure points, bent wrists and locked joints, usually for 45 minutes to an hour, and that many friends ended up with broken wrists.[12] The student said the on-campus psychiatrist prescribed medication from staff behavior reports without consulting the student or checking medical records, and that a Ritalin prescription led to a number of seizures.[12]

A former student who was there in 2008 and 2009 described the ranch on Unsilenced, a site that publishes survivor accounts.[4] The student said staff pinned teenagers face down when they refused orders.[4] The student said a staff member, Joe Giles, broke a boy's wrist taking him to the ground, and that the boy waited hours for care.[4]

On March 24, 2011, Aspen Education Group announced it would discontinue five programs, Aspen Ranch among them, and consolidate three others.[2] The company cited "the reduced demand for therapeutic schools and programs in today's economy."[2] Aspen Ranch closed that year; Aspen Achievement Academy, the company's wilderness program in the same town, was merged into Outback Therapeutic Expeditions.[2]
