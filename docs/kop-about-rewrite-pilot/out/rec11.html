Rewrite

Canyon State Academy is a residential treatment center for boys in Queen Creek, Arizona, run by Rite of Passage.[1] Until 2000 it was Arizona Boys Ranch, which by 1998 held more than 500 boys, about three-quarters of them placed by California.[2] Accusations that its staff hurt boys go back to at least 1971.[2] In 1998 a 16-year-old, Nicholaus Contreraz, died after staff punished him for complaining that he was sick; Arizona refused to renew the ranch's license and a grand jury indicted five staff members.[3][4] Under its new name, two students have died of drug overdoses since 2020, and in 2023 several staff members were charged with assaulting students.[5][6][7]

The Phoenix Rotary Club founded Arizona Boys Ranch as a nonprofit in 1949.[2] In 1971 it held 109 boys at its Queen Creek compound.[2] Robert B. Thomas, who came from a Michigan youth camp in 1977, ran it for the next two decades and built a hard-line, discipline-first program.[2] By 1998 it housed more than 500 boys on seven campuses across Arizona, including the Queen Creek compound and a boot camp near Oracle.[2] Its $26 million budget came mostly from taxpayers, and California's Department of Social Services paid for about three-quarters of the boys.[2]

The violence was reported from the start of that period. In 1971 the superintendent, Wendell Newell, was accused of punching boys, and the ranch's president answered that wayward youth could not be "handled by pantywaist methods".[2] A 1982 state report described staff grabbing boys by the throat and throwing them to the ground, and Maricopa County courts removed 19 boys from the ranch.[2] In 1987, after runaways reported abuse, a state Department of Corrections investigation supported Child Protective Services' findings; the ranch disputed them.[2] In 1994 Child Protective Services substantiated 13 incidents of abuse and suspended the ranch's license.[2] In 1995 Alameda County, California, withdrew its 67 boys after half of them said ranch staff had abused them, and the county's juvenile court concluded there was "systematic abuse".[8][2] That year the ranch fired two employees who had struck a 15-year-old California boy 25 to 30 times.[8] The ranch paid a team led by former U.S. Attorney Melvin McDonald about $400,000 to investigate, and the team called the abuse claims bogus.[2][4] In 1996 five state employees wrote in an internal memo that the ranch "continues to abuse children, thwart regulations and use their political influence" to fight licensing rules. That year the Department of Economic Security agreed to give the ranch 48 hours' notice before inspections.[8] The state put the ranch's license on provisional status for abuse three times. By mid-1998 nearly 100 child abuse complaints had been made against the ranch or its staff in five years, and state officials had substantiated 21.[8]

On June 27, 1994, 17-year-old Lorenzo Johnson ran from ranch employees while being driven from Queen Creek to Oracle. Cornered at a bridge, he went into the Central Arizona Project canal and drowned; his body was found seven days later, 15 miles downstream.[9] A Pinal County detective ruled the death accidental. His mother, Minnie Dunwoody, told the Arizona Republic, "I think they killed him, but why, I do not know."[9]

Nicholaus Contreraz, 16, was sent from California to the ranch's Oracle boot camp after stealing a car.[3][2] In his last weeks there he had chest pain, trouble breathing, diarrhea and constant vomiting, and lost 14 pounds in two months.[3][10] Staff called him a malingerer and punished him harder when he complained. The nurse, Linda Babb, kept sending him back to strenuous exercise.[3] Staff made him do push-ups over a bucket of his own soiled clothes and threw water on his face when he passed out.[10] He collapsed and died on March 2, 1998.[3] The medical examiner found empyema, an infection that had filled his chest cavity with pus, and his body had 71 cuts and bruises.[3][10] The ranch first said he died during recreation, and later acknowledged he had been mistreated.[2]

The state's response was the most serious the ranch had faced. On August 26, 1998, Linda Blessing, director of the Arizona Department of Economic Security (DES), refused to renew its license. DES found 17 employees, including supervisors, responsible for 32 instances of abuse or neglect of Contreraz, part of what investigators called "a pattern of abuse and neglect".[4] The state would put 17 former staff members on its Child Abuser Directory, and officials could not recall Arizona ever denying such a license before.[11] A California report found "medical neglect and physical abuse" and "widespread excessive use of physical restraint" at the ranch.[11] California cut off funding, and enrollment fell from nearly 600 boys at seven sites to 150.[4] On October 1, 1998, a Pinal County grand jury indicted Babb and four "work specialists", Geoffrey Sean Lewis, Montgomery Clayton Hoover, Michael Martin Moreno and Troy Michael Jones, each on one count of child abuse and one of manslaughter.[3] By September 2000 none of the five still faced charges. The last was the nurse, Linda Babb, whose charges prosecutors dropped after a key medical witness, Dr. Robert Kearl, reversed his testimony.[12] The ranch stayed shut only briefly: about two months after the denial, a settlement with the state let it reopen on condition that it adopt a "zero tolerance" policy on abuse and hire an ombudsman to advocate for the boys.[13] Nicholaus Contreraz's family settled a civil suit against the ranch for an estimated $1 million.[12]

In 2000 Arizona Boys Ranch was renamed Canyon State Academy. The East Valley Tribune reported in 2003 that the name was changed "to help improve its image after a student died amid allegations of abuse".[14] It is now operated by Rite of Passage, and the school is a nonprofit 501(c)(3) organization.[1] Complaints continued under the new name. In February 2003 a 26-year-old employee was placed on leave over allegations that she had sex with a 16-year-old student.[14] In 2014 Sycamore Canyon Academy in Oracle began housing unaccompanied migrant children, and Oracle residents reported runaways and a rise in violence.[15][16]

On January 21, 2020, a 17-year-old student was found unresponsive at Canyon State and died; the Maricopa County Sheriff's Office investigated, and the medical examiner later ruled that he died of a drug overdose.[17][18][5] On October 3, 2022, a 16-year-old student died of a suspected overdose and two others were hospitalized; early reports suggested family members may have supplied the drugs.[6][19][20] Officers had responded to the school 239 times between January 12 and October 4, 2022.[5] The school then faced questions over those calls, many about missing students and assaults. Former students described a punitive environment, and a foster parent called for closer state oversight.[21][22][23][24] In July 2023 police records and Department of Child Safety files detailed staff assaulting students during restraints, and several staff members were charged, some with aggravated assault.[7]
