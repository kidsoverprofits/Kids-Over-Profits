Rewrite

Cross Creek Programs, in La Verkin, Utah, ran a girls' program, Cross Creek Manor, and a boys' program, Cross Creek Center, as part of Robert Lichfield's network of behavior-modification programs, the World Wide Association of Specialty Programs and Schools (WWASPS).[1][2][3] Former students and parents have taken the programs to court since the 1990s.[4][5] One family's 2006 lawsuit against Cross Creek and WWASPS grew by 2011 into a case brought for 350 former students and 150 parents.[1][6] In 2013 a former resident sued Cross Creek Manor in federal court, alleging that it had held her in slavery.[2]

Cross Creek grew out of Provo Canyon School, where Lichfield had been director of residential living.[5][3] He opened Cross Creek Manor, a program for girls, in La Verkin in 1988 with his partner Brent Facer, and later added a boys' program; it was the oldest school in what became his network.[5][4] By 1992 it was using teen escort companies to transport students there.[7] Karr Farnsworth, who had also worked with Lichfield at Provo Canyon, was president of the network in 1999 and by 2003 ran Cross Creek's three programs.[4][5] In 1998 Lichfield and his attorney, the SkyWest Airlines founder J. Ralph Atkin, set up WWASPS as a nonprofit umbrella for the programs; Lichfield, Atkin and Facer were its first trustees.[3][5] Utah then had no way to license boarding schools.[3]

In 2003 Cross Creek was the network's only program that called itself a treatment center.[5] It charged up to $3,990 a month plus a $2,000 processing fee, and students stayed an average of 16 months.[5] Students had no television or internet, their phone calls were controlled and their mail was monitored, and they moved through six levels.[5] Its school was accredited by the Northwest Accreditation Commission.[8] Punishments included essays of 3,000 to 12,000 words and isolation rooms, which Farnsworth said were rarely used.[5] "Some students and parents say they have been abused in World Wide schools -- forced to kneel for hours on concrete, physically restrained by staff and made to perform menial labor," The Salt Lake Tribune reported in 2003.[5] By 1999 the attorney Thomas Burton had filed two federal cases against WWASPS schools, and by 2003 he had brought seven suits against WWASPS and its programs, four of which had been dismissed.[4][5]

Cross Creek Manor's own parent manual, signed by Farnsworth as administrator, shows how the program cut girls off from home.[9] A girl's first phone call with her parents came after two months, and calls after that were scheduled twice a month in therapy sessions.[9] Parents could not visit until she had been there 12 weeks, and home visits were not usually considered before six months.[9] All of her mail, in both directions, was sent to her parents to screen and forward.[9] Parents were told that a daughter's complaints were manipulation, and that if a complaint bothered them they should not let her know it.[9] The enrollment contract set a six-month minimum and charged three months' fees for withdrawing early without notice.[9] It let staff search a girl's person, physically restrain and detain her, and confine her to a "special needs room" under observation.[9] It also barred her, after she left, from living or going to school within 50 miles of the program until she turned 18, unless parents kept paying the monthly fee.[9] Punishments included fines against a girl's allowance and essays she had to write alone in a study carrel, apart from the program, until they were done.[9] Medication was self-administered under the supervision of non-medical staff.[9]

Former students and a former staff member have published their own accounts of Cross Creek since 2013 (see Survivor testimony below).[10][11][12] In a March 2021 Vice News video, former students alleged that a male staff member supervised a 13-year-old girl in the shower and that male staff staged rape re-enactments with girls who had been sexually abused; program executives denied the allegations.[13]

On August 25, 2006, William Chase Wood and his parents sued WWASPS, Cross Creek Center for Boys, Cross Creek Manor, Lichfield, Ken Kay, Karr Farnsworth and others.[1] They alleged physical, emotional and sexual abuse, confinement and forced labor during about two years at Cross Creek and High Impact, and racketeering in how the programs were sold to parents.[1] On October 13, 2006, 25 more former students and their families joined the suit in an amended complaint.[14] The federal court dismissed the racketeering claims.[6][15] In 2011 the case was refiled in state court on behalf of 350 former students and 150 parents, who alleged beatings, sexual abuse and being made to lie in urine and feces as punishment.[6][15] In 2012 it was moved to the Fifth District Court in St. George, where 38 of the 57 defendants lived.[15]

In 2013 Sarah Artim and her mother, Nancy, sued Cross Creek Manor and WWASPS in federal court.[2] Sarah had been placed there at 15, in about January 2007, for anorexia, obsessive-compulsive disorder and anxiety, and stayed about 16 months.[2] The suit alleged that the enrollment agreement made her a "mindless slave": it handed her custody to the program and allowed strip searches and physical restraint.[2] It also alleged forced manual labor and isolation rooms used as punishment, behind tall fences that kept parents from seeing much.[2] Nancy Artim said the roughly $100,000 in fees drove her into bankruptcy.[2] The La Verkin site later became a motel, with part of the old facility closed off and left abandoned.[16]
