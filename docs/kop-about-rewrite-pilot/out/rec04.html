Rewrite

Eagle Ranch Academy is a licensed residential treatment center for teenagers at 115 West 1470 South in St. George, Utah, with room for 57 young people.[1] It was founded by the brothers Paul and David Arslanian, former Utah State University football coaches.[2][3] Police logged calls to the academy reporting assaults, child abuse and runaways from 2006 on, and former students have described isolation, painful restraints and humiliating group seminars there in accounts going back to at least 2014, and in 2020 a parent wrote that her daughter was restrained in a neck hold on her first day.[4][5][6]

David Arslanian was Utah State's head football coach in 1998 and 1999.[3] He hired his brother Paul as a coordinator over the university president's objection, was fired on December 2, 1999, and sued the university in 2000.[3] By 2013 Woodbury Reports, an industry newsletter, listed David as the academy's co-founder and director of admissions.[2] Paul Arslanian was the person on site when state licensers inspected the program in August 2025.[1] Woodbury Reports listed 1995 as the program's start year.[2]

In 2005 the program sought land-use permits in two southern Utah counties.[7][8] Seven years earlier the Salt Lake Tribune had reported that Hyrum Smith, the founder of Franklin Quest, owned the D.I. Ranch, an 831-acre cattle ranch 25 miles west of St. George, and planned to sell it to investors who wanted to turn it into a wilderness retreat for troubled youths.[9] On April 26, 2005, the Washington County Planning Commission took up a request to run a residential treatment facility and school for 20 youths at "Eagle Ranch Academy, Inc. (a.k.a. DI Ranch)," with Dave Arslanian as one of the applicant's agents.[7] The commission's chairman said the applicants were not prepared and needed to provide much more information, and the request was tabled.[7] In May 2005 the Beaver County Commission, after hearing Dave Arslanian, unanimously upheld its planning commission's denial of a permit for the academy in the county's "grove" area.[8] On June 7, 2005, the Washington County Commission approved the D.I. Ranch permit for 20 youths or fewer, on the applicant's statements that each youth would stay six to ten months and that no adjudicated youth would be admitted.[10] The St. George Area Chamber of Commerce later listed Doug Caputo as the contact for D.I. Ranch LLC, also known as Kayenta Homes & Properties.[11]

The academy's 2009 enrollment packet told parents it was licensed by the Utah Department of Human Services.[12] It bound families to a minimum stay of six months while reserving the program's right to dismiss a student at any time, and it authorized staff to restrain students with Positive Control Systems techniques.[12] A parent wrote in a 2018 Google review that the program's home page falsely claimed accreditation by SAIS and SACSCOC, and that both agencies confirmed it held neither.[13] Another parent wrote in 2017 that it charged insurers $800 a day.[14] The program has publicly denied any association with WWASP.[15] A parent who pulled a son out wrote in a 2015 Google review that the seminar and the level system said otherwise.[16]

State licensers have repeatedly found the program out of compliance.[17][18][19] In March 2024 an investigation substantiated that staff had left clients unsupervised for longer than 15 minutes, during which they engaged in unsafe behaviors, a repeat violation.[17] That May licensers found the program had not reported a May 4 critical incident until May 7, also a repeat violation.[18] On November 17, 2025, an investigation found it out of compliance with the rules requiring providers to protect clients from abuse and to follow safe practices.[19]

Complaints about the academy reach back years before the survivors' posts.[4] St. George police records of incidents at its address from 2006 to 2013 include reports of sexual offenses, child abuse and children found unconscious, along with assaults and runaway juveniles.[4] A recording of a December 2009 conversation between a student and the student's parents at the academy was later published online.[20] HEAL, the site that published it, said the student described cult-like seminars, beatings, drugging with undisclosed psychotropic medication and humiliating punishments that included spitting on children.[20]

Former students posting on the survivor site WWASP Survivors describe the regime from the inside.[5] One wrote in March 2014 that new students were denied shoes and socks and made to walk on hot pavement, that students were isolated in rooms for days or "ghosted" for months with no contact except staff, and that staff restrained children until their arms bled.[5] Another, writing that September, described being made to stand before about 20 people in a seminar and be "verbally dismantled," being put on antipsychotics and antidepressants without a choice, and being denied contact with parents at the start.[5] A survivor who spent seven months there wrote in September 2020 of watching children restrained by several adults at once and forced into isolation for months.[5]

In January 2020 a mother wrote on Yelp that her daughter was assaulted on her first day and then restrained by an adult man who dragged her down a hall in a neck hold and put her face down on the floor; she said her husband watched the video with police, and that her daughter slept on the floor for 14 days.[6]
