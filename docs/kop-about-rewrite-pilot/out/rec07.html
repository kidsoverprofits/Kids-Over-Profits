Rewrite

The Family Foundation School was a boarding school for teenagers in Hancock, New York, founded by Tony and Betty Argiros and run by their family until it closed in 2014.[1][2][3] Former students made its abuse public years before it closed. In 2008 a former student told a congressional committee that staff and students restrained children by wrapping them in duct tape and blankets, and the school said it would not dispute his testimony.[4][5] In 2018 The New York Times reported that at least 90 alumni had died since the school closed, mostly of overdoses or suicide.[6] In 2025 a federal jury convicted Paul Geer, a longtime choir teacher there, of taking students across state lines to abuse them.[7] In 2026 a former student sued the family, the village of Hancock and its police for $10 million.[8]

By 2011 the founders' children and grandchildren ran the school, and Mike Argiros was executive director.[2][9] Students lived in "family houses" overseen by staff called family leaders, followed a twelve-step program, and most stayed 18 months to two years or longer.[10][11] In its 2009 listing with the National Association of Therapeutic Schools and Programs, the school reported 200 students aged 12 to 18 and a minimum stay of 18 months, gave its founding year as 1979, and said it had been a member since 1999.[12]

Former students have described the same abuse for decades. Jon Martin-Crawford, held there from 1995 to 1997, testified to the House Committee on Education and Labor on April 24, 2008.[4] He said typical restraints meant wrapping children in duct tape and blankets, sometimes in a six-by-six isolation room, and that he saw staff punch students in the face while restraining them.[4] Students as well as staff were told to chase, tackle and restrain children who ran away, and children were forced to eat food they were allergic to and keep eating even while vomiting.[4] In a statement on May 11, 2008, the school said it would not dispute his testimony. It said that in the 1990s it had modeled restraint on first aid for seizures, "by using blankets," and that after a New York State Education Department review it switched to a "quiet room" and, from 1999, trained staff in Therapeutic Crisis Intervention.[5] In June 2010 the state Commission on Quality of Care and Advocacy for Persons with Disabilities made a two-day visit after complaints. By the school's own account, written by program director Rita Argiros, the commission found "no abuse or neglect" but raised "a number of concerns."[13] On March 4, 2004, a 17-year-old student died at the school in what it reported as a suicide.[14]

In October 2013 the school renamed itself Allynwood Academy.[15] On July 31, 2014, it told referral sources it would close on August 8, citing "the financial realities that we are facing."[3] In September 2018 The New York Times reported on its front page that at least 90 alumni had died since the closing, mostly of overdoses or suicide. It said the school had closed after enrollment fell in the wake of a "truth campaign" in which alumni described solitary confinement, enforced silence and restraint.[6] Elizabeth "Liz" Ianelli, an alumna who led the count, took the name Survivor993 from the number of days she spent there.[6][16] She told her story in the 2023 memoir "I See You, Survivor" and in the 2026 documentary "The Kids Are Not Alright."[16][17]

Paul Geer taught choir at the school from 1991 until it closed and was also a family leader.[18][19][20] In April 2024 a federal indictment charged him with six counts involving three students: three of coercing and enticing them to travel across state lines for sexual activity, and three of transporting them across state lines for that purpose.[21][22] He had taken them to Maine, Pennsylvania and Toronto in 1994, 2000 and 2001.[22] On March 3, 2025, an Albany jury convicted him on the four counts involving two of the students and could not agree on the third.[22][7] The trial evidence showed he denied students food, forced them to eat regurgitated food, bound children in rugs and isolated them.[22][7] On September 4, 2025, he was sentenced to 27 years and three months in federal prison. Prosecutors said the punishments he imposed were "tantamount to torture".[23][24]

Lawsuits followed the school's closing. In June 2026 a man who was a student from 2000 to 2003 sued the Argiros family, the village of Hancock and local police in federal court in Brooklyn, seeking $10 million.[8] He alleges students were strip-searched, locked in closets and made to dig their own graves, and that he was made to eat vomit.[8][25] He claims local authorities ignored reports of abuse because of the owners' influence.[8][25] Michael Argiros has denied knowing of abuse allegations while he ran the school.[26]
