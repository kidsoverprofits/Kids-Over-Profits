Rewrite

Aspen Education Group, based in Cerritos, California, was one of the largest owners of troubled-teen programs in the United States. It ran wilderness programs, therapeutic boarding schools, residential treatment centers and the Wellspring weight-loss camps.[1] Backed by private equity from its start, it grew to 32 programs in 12 states by early 2006, and its chief executive said it took in about 4,000 children in 2005.[2] In 2006 CRC Health Group, which Bain Capital had just bought, paid $296.4 million for it.[3] Children died in its programs throughout its history: Matthew Meyer, 14, at Lone Star Expeditions in Texas in 2004; Brendan Blum, 14, at Youth Care of Utah in 2007; and Sergey Blashchishen, 16, at SageWalk in Oregon in 2009.[4][5][6] In 2009 Oregon found abuse and neglect at its Mount Bachelor Academy, and from 2011 to 2013 Aspen announced the closing of ten more programs.[7][8][9]

The company's own account dates its start as Aspen Education Group to 1998, and its co-founder and chief executive was Elliot Sainer.[2] It grew fast, from six programs in 1998 to 31 in 2005.[10] Its largest purchase came on November 30, 2004, when it announced it had bought two Utah programs, Island View and the Oakley School, which Lorin Broadbent and Jared Balmer had founded; the founders stayed on to run them.[11] On January 31, 2006, Bain Capital bought CRC Health, the country's largest for-profit addiction treatment chain, in a leveraged buyout valued at $723 million.[3][4] At the end of September 2006 CRC bought Aspen for $296.4 million, about twice its annual revenue, and Aspen became part of CRC.[3][1] In 2014 Acadia Healthcare agreed to buy CRC in a stock deal valued at about $1.175 billion.[12]

Deaths span the company's history. Matthew Meyer died of heat stroke in 2004, eight days into his stay at Lone Star Expeditions; his family's wrongful-death suit was settled.[4] On June 28, 2007, Brendan Blum died at Youth Care of Utah in Draper. He had been vomiting and had diarrhea, but staff did not call the on-call nurse; an autopsy found a twisted bowel had cut off blood to his small intestine. Prosecutors charged two counselors with felony child abuse or neglect, and the state put the program on probation for inadequate medical care without fining it.[5] A Utah court dismissed the charges in 2008, and his mother settled a wrongful-death suit.[4] In 2008 a 16-year-old at Adirondack Leadership Expeditions in New York suffered frostbite severe enough to need eight months of treatment.[4] On August 28, 2009, Sergey Blashchishen died of apparent heat stroke on his first hike at SageWalk. A Lake County sheriff's deputy wrote that staff actions could amount to criminal mistreatment, and that SageWalk "may have taken children to the precipice of disaster and been lucky."[6]

Also in 2009, a seven-month Oregon investigation found nine cases of abuse or neglect involving five students at Mount Bachelor Academy since 2007. Most involved a mandatory program called Lifesteps, in which at least two students were made to act out sexual scenarios in front of staff and peers.[7] The state gave the school 90 days to fix the problems or lose its license, and it closed that year. CRC said the allegations were false.[7][4] In July 2011 nine former students, all enrolled as teenagers in the 1990s, sued in Multnomah County, Oregon, seeking $14 million.[13] They alleged that untrained staff berated them and punished them physically, that "Lifestep" sessions ran for days with sleep and food deprivation, and that a girl who had been sexually abused was forced into a sexually humiliating exercise in front of male students and staff.[14]

Closures followed in waves. On March 24, 2011, Aspen announced that it would close five more and consolidate three, blaming "reduced demand for therapeutic schools and programs in today's economy." The five were Bromley Brook School, New Leaf Academy of Oregon, NorthStar Center, Aspen Ranch and SunHawk Adolescent Recovery Center.[8] On July 10, 2013, it announced five more: the Academy at Swift River, Talisman Academy, Stone Mountain School, SUWS Adolescent & Youth Programs in Idaho, and Adirondack Leadership Expeditions.[9] CRC's chief executive, Andrew Eckert, told employees that "gone are the days of student loans for these programs."[15] That August the Oakley School in Utah, which Aspen had owned since 2004, began a partnership with InnerChange.[16]

The sales led to lawsuits. In 2014 Terri and David Myers of Missouri sued Aspen, Island View, CRC Health Group, Bain Capital, the Dr. Phil organization and an Island View teacher, Ryan Mortensen. They said Dr. Phil had recommended Island View for their 15-year-old daughter after a 2013 broadcast, and that her arm was badly broken when Mortensen pulled her off her bed.[17]
