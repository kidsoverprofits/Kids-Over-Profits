Rewrite

Mount Bachelor Academy was a therapeutic boarding school for teenagers about 26 miles east of Prineville, Oregon, owned by Aspen Education Group.[1] It took its first students in 1988 and by 2009 charged $6,400 a month.[2][3] Complaints that it mistreated students reached the press as early as 1998.[4] In November 2009, after a seven-month investigation, Oregon found that students had been sexually humiliated and abused in its "Lifesteps" seminars and suspended its license, and the school closed for good that December.[5][1][3] Former students then brought three lawsuits over abuse going back to the 1990s, which ended in confidential settlements in 2014.[6][7]

By the school's own account, Barry Weiss of College Health Enterprises, a group of Southern California hospitals, approached the educator Linda Houghton in 1987 about starting an emotional-growth boarding school.[2][8] After permit trouble in Powell Butte, the school was built at the former Mark's Creek Lodge in the Ochoco National Forest.[2] It took its first students in 1988, with Houghton as executive director, and graduated its first class in December 1990.[2] Its methods came from CEDU, the California school whose founder, Mel Wasserman, drew on the Synanon movement, and Lifesteps workshops were part of its program from the start.[8][2] Tim Brace was executive director from 1993 to 1997.[2][1] Sharon Bitz, who had worked at the school for eight years, took over in May 1997 and ran it until it closed.[2][1] In 1998 College Health Enterprises spun the school and other youth programs off into Aspen Youth Services, later renamed Aspen Education Group.[2][8] In 2006 CRC Health Group, owned by the private equity firm Bain Capital, bought Aspen.[8][9] Oregon's Department of Human Services licensed the school.[10] Its academic program was accredited by the Northwest Association of Accredited Schools.[11]

In 1998 the Associated Press reported that former employees and parents said teens were mistreated at the academy.[4] That March four former employees and two parents alleged that students were deprived of sleep, subjected to obscenity-laced tirades and made to work overnight in near-freezing temperatures.[4] The Oregon Department of Human Services investigated and cleared the program.[12] In December 1998 a 17-year-old student, Brandon H., died outside the facility; the Crook County sheriff and coroner investigated and ruled his death a suicide.[13] By 2009 the school was licensed for 125 students and had 88, with 77 staff.[12][3] Students were 14 to 17½ years old and typically stayed 14 to 16 months.[12][3]

The state's investigation began after complaints in the spring of 2009.[5][8] Students who spoke to the writer Maia Szalavitz for an April 2009 TIME story said that in Lifesteps seminars girls, some of them survivors of sexual abuse, were made to dress provocatively and give lap dances to boys; one girl said she was made to dress as a "French maid".[12][14][15] Bitz called the reports "inaccurate representations" and said some accusations were "demonstrably false".[12] On November 3, 2009, the Department of Human Services confirmed nine allegations of abuse and neglect involving five students since 2007, most of them in Lifesteps.[5][16][1] Investigators found sexualized role-play in front of other students, students made to re-enact past physical and sexual abuse, sleep deprivation, manual labor and forced exercise as punishment, and bans on speaking and on phone calls home.[8][5][16] They called the Lifesteps workshops "punitive, humiliating, degrading and traumatizing".[8] The school had one Oregon-licensed mental health professional and no staff qualified to treat substance abuse or eating disorders.[1] The state suspended its license, gave it 90 days to correct the violations and filed a complaint against Bitz, saying she knew or should have known of the abuse.[1][3]

Aspen did not try to reopen.[3] On November 9, 2009, it announced the school would close permanently by December 9, eliminating 69 to 72 jobs.[3] It blamed "the revocation of the Facility's operating license," though the state said it had only suspended the license.[3] The school and Bitz fought the findings in four legal proceedings from December 2009 to July 2010.[17] They settled in October 2010.[17] The department replaced "substantiated" in its findings with a statement that it had "reasonable cause to believe that abuse or neglect occurred," and the school acknowledged the department had a reasonable basis to investigate.[17] The findings against Bitz would be withdrawn only if she completed a two-year course in child and adolescent psychology.[17] A department spokesman, Gene Evans, said, "The settlement does not exonerate MBA."[15]

On July 6, 2011, nine former students sued the school, Aspen and CRC Health in Multnomah County for $14.25 million, alleging emotional, physical and sexual abuse in the 1990s.[6][18][19][15] They described sexual role-play, forced labor, sleep deprivation, exposure to extreme cold, and denial of food, bathroom access and medical care.[18][19][15] The state had recovered bedsheets bearing sexual slurs such as "whore" and "slut".[15] The school denied the claims and said it had provided a nurturing environment.[20] By late August 2011 the suit had 17 plaintiffs seeking $25.5 million, and more former students sued that November.[21][22][23] The November suit, brought by 14 former students, sought $23 million.[23] A third suit followed in 2012.[7] In all, 51 former students brought the three suits, seeking more than $48 million, and all three were moved to Crook County Circuit Court.[7] A 2009 Oregon law had raised the age limit for child sexual abuse claims from 24 to 40, and in January 2013 a judge let one case go forward despite a statute-of-limitations defense.[7] The suits were resolved privately under confidential terms and dismissed in September and November 2014.[7]
