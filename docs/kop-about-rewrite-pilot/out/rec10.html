Rewrite

Midwest Academy was a private, for-profit boarding school for teenagers near Keokuk, Iowa, that operated for more than a decade until state and federal agents raided it in January 2016.[1][2][3] Its campus belonged to companies tied to Robert Lichfield, founder of the World Wide Association of Specialty Programs and Schools (WWASPS).[1][4][5] For years, former students say, children were held for days or weeks in small isolation rooms; six who attended between 2008 and 2015 described them to the Associated Press in 2016.[6] No state agency inspected the school in its 13 years.[7] In December 2017 a jury convicted its owner, Benjamin Trane, of assault with intent to commit sexual abuse of a student, sexual exploitation by a counselor and endangering children held in isolation, and he was sentenced to nine years in prison.[8][9]

The Iowa Supreme Court has said Trane and his wife opened the school in 2002 and that he was its owner and de facto director.[2] A local history dates the start to March 2003, when the Lichfield family partnership bought Charleston Place, a former senior living home on the edge of Keokuk, for $500,000, and Trane and Brian Vaifanua opened the academy there.[1] Midwest Twister LLC, a Utah company, bought the property in 2006 and leased it to Trane; it denied any other connection to the school in court filings.[5][4] The academy also ran the Midwest Treatment Center in nearby Montrose.[10] Families paid about $5,000 a month.[6] Because it was privately funded and took no state placements, it needed no license, and the Iowa Department of Human Services never licensed it.[6][10] A later Iowa Department of Education review found it had misstated its accreditations and affiliations.[7]

Former students describe a regime built on isolation. The rooms, which the school called OSS, were meant for one student at a time; the lights stayed on, cameras watched, and speakers played screams and motivational recordings.[2][6][11] A student could leave only after sitting "in structure," in a set posture, for 24 hours.[6][11] New arrivals were barred from talking to other students or to staff.[11] The Iowa Supreme Court recounted that one boy spent at least 133 of his 210 days at the school in isolation and dropped from 115 to 89 pounds, and another spent about half his time there and went from 120 to 90 pounds.[2] Students also described self-harm, a suicide attempt and parents' contact limited and monitored.[6] A 2016 Des Moines Register review found scores of police calls to the academy over three years, at least five of them alleging sexual abuse.[7]

The school's collapse began in late 2015. On December 1, 2015, Cheyenne Jerred, a night supervisor, reported to her superiors and to Human Services a student's allegation that Trane had sexually assaulted her; the academy fired Jerred four days later, and she sued in January 2016.[12] On January 28, 2016, the Iowa Division of Criminal Investigation, the FBI and other agencies served search warrants at the Keokuk campus and the Montrose center.[10][6] Human Services opened child abuse assessments of 28 students, and the school's roughly 90 students were removed.[10][6] In February the Lee County sheriff's office said 19 abuse accusations it had investigated were founded.[1] The academy laid off its staff and never reopened.[12]

The case showed how little power Iowa had over such schools. In March 2016 a Human Services administrator told legislators the department had nothing in law that let it shut a facility down.[13] In April 2016 the Iowa House passed a bill, prompted by the Midwest Academy investigation, requiring the state to certify and inspect private children's residential facilities, with an exemption for the curricula of religious organizations.[14] It became law that year as Iowa Code chapter 237C, which sets standards for abuse protection, background checks, and seclusion and restraint.[15]

Former students and staff took the school to court. In March 2016 six former students who attended between November 2010 and September 2015, and their parents, sued the academy, Trane and associated companies, alleging isolation boxes, sexual assault, poor nutrition and medical care, and false claims of accreditation.[16] In March 2019 a judge dismissed Midwest Twister from the case, finding nothing tied it to the school beyond owning the land, and about half the plaintiffs later asked to withdraw.[17] Cheyenne Jerred did better: in May 2017 Judge John Wright awarded her $748,800 against the academy, which had defaulted.[18] Trane told her lawyer that December that he had "walked away from the school with nothing," having signed its assets over to Midwest Twister and another Lichfield company after the raid.[4] The campus itself went to a county tax auction in June 2016 over unpaid property taxes, and in February 2019 the tax-certificate buyer, Oak Helm Partners, moved to take ownership.[5] Midwest Academy was also caught up in the long-running Utah suit against the Lichfield network. In Wood v. WWASPS, former students named Midwest Academy, L.L.C. as a defendant and called it a school associated with WWASPS.[19] Their amended complaint said Iowa records listed Brian Viafanua as its owner. It also named a Midwest Outsource Services, L.L.C. whose listed member was Robert B. Lichfield. And it cited a 2004 email exchange in which, the plaintiffs said, WWASPS's Ken Kay worked behind the scenes so that Midwest would not appear connected to the network.[19]

Trane was charged in September 2017, after a 19-month investigation, with third-degree sexual abuse, sexual exploitation by a counselor and child endangerment.[3] Prosecutors said that during 2015 he pressured a student into sexual acts by tying them to her progress through the levels and to contact with her family, and had girls undress before a mirror in what was called "body image therapy."[3] On December 22, 2017, a jury convicted him of the lesser offense of assault with intent to commit sexual abuse, along with sexual exploitation by a counselor and child endangerment.[8] In May 2018 Judge Mark Kruse gave him the maximum nine years and ordered him to register as a sex offender, saying Trane's claims of innocence had "revictimized the victims."[9] In 2019 the Iowa Supreme Court ordered a hearing on whether the girl had made false accusations before; the trial judge found she had not.[7] The Iowa Supreme Court upheld the conviction in January 2023.[7] In January 2025 it reversed a lower court's order granting him a new trial on the child endangerment count, holding that a flawed jury instruction had not prejudiced him.[2]
