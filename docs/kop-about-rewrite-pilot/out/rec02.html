Rewrite

Provo Canyon School is a locked residential treatment center for teenagers in Utah County, Utah, founded in 1971 and owned since 2000 by Universal Health Services (UHS), one of the country's largest for-profit behavioral health companies.[1][2] Abuse allegations have followed it for five decades.[3][4] In 1980 a federal judge barred the school from polygraphing boys, censoring their mail and isolating them; in 2020 The Salt Lake Tribune found "a pattern of controversy and allegations of abuse that stretches from the 1980s to today"; and between 2015 and 2020 state licensers opened 341 investigations at its campuses.[3][4] Paris Hilton's 2020 documentary brought survivors' accounts to a national audience.[4][5][6][7] After a run of injuries to teenagers, Utah revoked the licenses of both campuses in July 2026 and ordered all services stopped by August 16; UHS's appeal is pending.[4][5][6][7]

Provo Canyon opened as a school for boys in 1971, owned by Jack Williams and Robert H. Crist; D. Eugene Thorne later joined them as an owner.[1][4][3] Thorne was a co-owner from 1978 to 1986.[8] In 1986 the psychiatric hospital chain Charter bought the original Provo campus through a subsidiary, Charter Provo School, Inc., and owned the school for about 14 years; court papers name Charter Medical Corporation as the parent.[4][9] Charter Behavioral Health Systems went bankrupt in 2000 amid Medicaid fraud allegations and reports of inadequate care.[4] In August 2000 UHS bought 11 of its behavioral health facilities, Provo Canyon School among them.[2] Girls were held at campuses in Orem and, later, Springville.[4] By 2026 the school ran a boys' campus in Provo and the girls' campus in Springville.[1]

The school was the model for much of what followed.[1] It "spurred an avalanche of imitators," APM Reports wrote in 2026, and helped make Utah "the epicenter of what is often called the troubled-teen industry."[1] Robert B. Lichfield, who went on to found the World Wide Association of Specialty Programs and Schools (WWASP), started at Provo Canyon as a dorm parent in 1977 and worked there for years in several roles.[10][11]

Eight former students whose stays spanned decades described the school to The Salt Lake Tribune in 2020.[4] "They spoke of repeated physical restraints, with up to 10 staffers piling on young children," the paper reported.[4] Jeremy Whiteley, who enrolled in 1989 at 15, said: "I went willingly. My dad took me. And basically, after I said goodbye, that's when the nightmare started."[4] He called his stay "basically almost two years of prison."[4] Kyra Lewis, who arrived in 2003, said physical restraints "happened all the time"; "injections also were common and had been nicknamed 'booty juice,'" the Tribune wrote.[4] Jen Robison, who also arrived in 2003, said: "The thing that still haunts me is the amount of time in isolation, being left in that room for so long, watching other girls being restrained and shot up with Haldol."[4] The school's own parent manual from the 1996–97 school year described the system in milder terms.[12] New students could write letters home, but their phone calls and visits were restricted during an orientation phase.[12] Students who broke rules could be moved for hours or days to the "Investment Unit," a secured area where they earned back privileges in 25-minute periods of sitting quietly.[12] A student "temporarily out of control" could be put in a seclusion room, "a small, empty room."[12]

The first legal challenge came from two boys the school held for other states' juvenile courts.[3] On September 21, 1978, Timothy Milonas Jr., sent by a Nevada court, and Kenneth Rice, sent by an Alaska court, filed a federal class action against the owners, Williams, Crist and later Thorne.[3] The court found that the school read and censored boys' mail, gave them polygraph tests, locked them in isolation rooms and used excessive force, including the "hair dance," a grip on a boy's hair.[3] In 1980 a jury found for the owners on damages, but the judge permanently barred polygraphs and mail opening, and allowed isolation and physical restraint only to contain a violent boy or in immediate danger.[4][3] On September 13, 1982, the Tenth Circuit upheld the injunction.[3] It ruled that the state was so involved in the school that it was "a joint participant," and that the practices violated the boys' due process and First Amendment rights.[3]

Complaints continued under Charter.[13] In September 1990 the school suspended a staff member accused of having sex with a 15-year-old girl four times, and Provo police looked into claims involving other students.[13] David Taylor, admitted from October 1990 to August 1991, sued in 1995 for fraud, medical negligence and false imprisonment.[9] A jury found the school 25 percent at fault and it was held liable for $7,500.[9] On December 9, 1998, the Fifth Circuit ruled that, unlike in the Milonas case, the school under Charter was not a state actor.[9] Paris Hilton was sent to the school at 17 in 1999 and stayed 11 months.[4][14] "That was the worst of the worst," she said in her 2020 documentary. "There's no getting out of there. You're sitting on a chair and staring at a wall all day long, getting yelled at or getting hit."[4] Of the drugs staff gave her, she said: "I didn't know what they were giving me."[4] The Associated Press summed up her account as staff who would "beat her, force her to take unknown pills, watch her shower and send her to solitary confinement without clothes as punishment."[14]

The accounts continued after UHS took over.[4] Six women who were there between 2003 and 2017 told The Salt Lake Tribune "similar stories of being overmedicated, restrained and punished for minor infractions."[4] Former students and parents have also published their own accounts of the school since 2004 (see Survivor testimony below).[15][16][17] In December 2009 the school sued the advocacy group HEAL for libel in Utah's Fourth District Court.[18]

In the 2010s the state began restricting the school's licenses.[19] On May 20, 2012, a 17-year-old boy who had walked away from the Provo campus stole an SUV and, fleeing police at more than 100 mph, hit another car and killed a 65-year-old woman.[19] Utah put the Provo campus on conditional status.[4] It did the same to the Springville campus in 2013 after a girl was injured in a restraint, and to the Provo campus again in 2015 after staff injured students in restraints twice; Provo was also cited for a locked seclusion room.[4] On January 3, 2017, the parents of a former resident sued the school for negligence and intentional infliction of emotional distress.[20] They alleged that staff denied their daughter medical care for nearly two weeks after she broke her thumb, and gave her staples, pens and plastic cutlery though staff knew she compulsively harmed herself.[20] In December 2017 staff and students punched each other in an unofficial "fight club."[21] In May 2018 a former staff member, Misifoa Tautofi Seui Matagi, 21, was charged with felony child abuse for hitting a 16-year-old boy there.[21] During stays in 2018 and 2019, a 14-year-old girl from Oregon's foster care system was pinned down nearly 30 times and injected with sedatives 17 times, The Salt Lake Tribune reported.[22] From 2015 to 2020 state licensers ran 341 investigations at the school's four campuses and substantiated 27 violations.[4][23] From January 2016 to June 2020 police answered 160 emergency calls at the Springville campus.[4][23]

In September 2020 Hilton described the abuse she suffered there in her YouTube documentary "This Is Paris."[23] With the survivor group Breaking Code Silence, she launched a petition to close the school, which passed 45,000 signatures within days.[23] By August 2022 the petition had passed 206,000 signatures.[24] On October 9, 2020, she led several hundred survivors to the school's front gates.[14] The school said it could not comment on anything before its owners bought it in 2000.[14] On February 8, 2021, Hilton testified for Senate Bill 127, which allowed surprise inspections of youth treatment centers and required them to report restraints.[25][22][26] The bill passed the legislature that March.[25][22][26] In August 2021 Aaron Ross, who was sent there at 13 in 2001, sued the school, UHS and three staff members in federal court in New York.[27][28] He alleged that staff member Justin Uale forced him to perform oral sex about a dozen times under threat of starvation.[27] He also said he was held for days or weeks in a bare "observation room," given Seroquel, Depakote, Trileptal and Celexa without a diagnosis, and kept indoors for two years.[27] The case was moved to federal court in Utah in January 2022.[28] On February 27, 2023, the court dismissed it for failure to prosecute.[28]

The violence continued.[29] On March 27, 2023, a resident at the Springville campus punched a staff member, who later died after surgery; police had been called to the campus eight times between February and April.[29] In September 2024 staff member Mulimauga Tuiloma punched a 15-year-old girl in the face and told police he had been using a restraint technique.[30][31] He pleaded guilty to child abuse, and in July 2025 he was given a suspended jail sentence and probation.[30][31] State licensers found that staff harmed residents in September 2024, restrained one unnecessarily that November, and struck a teenager being restrained in December.[32] It was the seventh time in two years that they had found the Springville program failed to keep young people safe, and in February 2025 they put conditions on its license, which covered 120 residents.[32] On April 13, 2025, licensers later found, a staff member at the Springville campus who did not follow the school's Handle With Care restraint policy injured a client's face during a restraint.[33] In February 2026 a staff member punched a client, who needed stitches in the mouth.[1] In January 2026 officials said a staff member struck a child being restrained, and staff told investigators they hesitated to put restraint details in writing "since those documents are public."[1]

Then came the incident that led to the school's closing.[34][35] On May 18, 2026, a 13-year-old boy at the Provo campus was knocked unconscious in a fight and suffered a broken jaw and a brain bleed.[34][35] Staff drove him to the hospital instead of calling 911, delaying his treatment by about an hour.[34][35] On May 29 the state took emergency action and barred new admissions.[34][36][37] On June 18 it put 10 conditions on the Provo license, finding that staff had failed to protect the boy, called a non-medical transport company instead of 911, and ignored his and his guardian's earlier safety concerns and transfer requests.[34][36][37] The boy's mother sued on June 15, saying staff ignored his warnings that he was not safe, and a second family sued over medical malpractice.[38][39] That June a staff member lay on a client's legs during a restraint and fractured the client's foot.[40] Utah's Disability Law Center visited both campuses and reported that teens were not safe there, citing violence, self-harm, poor medical care and chronic understaffing.[40] That month complaint investigations produced 22 findings at the Springville campus and 16 at the Provo campus.[41][42]

On July 6, 2026, Utah's Department of Health and Human Services revoked the Springville campus license and ordered the girls' program closed by August 6.[5][1][43] It cited understaffing, unnecessary restraint and aggressive physical contact, neglected care and incomplete background checks, and barred the owners from reapplying for five years.[5][1][43] On July 17 it revoked the Provo campus license and ordered all services there stopped by August 16, citing failures to protect clients from harm and the use of cruel and unnecessary practices.[6][44][45][46][47] UHS appealed both orders on July 27; the appeal did not lift the August 16 deadline, and it was still pending in August.[6][7] Hilton said the state had "confirmed what survivors have known all along," and in August she urged Utah not to let the school reopen.[5][48]

Survivor testimony

As a licensed professional, Registered Nurse, under duty of obligation under the laws of California I am writing this to document and report suspected and actual child abuse both physical, emotional, psychological, sexual as well as the suspected and actual abuse of children in the custody and care of (staff) at Provo Canyon School. … SUMMARY OF ABUSE SUFFERED BY OUR CHILD: Assault, battery, hair pulled, restrained against her will, drugged with an overdose of Haldol which caused her to black out and wake to blindness, facial paralysis and pain, drooling and lethargy for three days. Imprisoned and forced to sit in structure (staring straight ahead) for up to 5 hours. … We were told by cell-phone message on or about January 10, that the case was going to be closed because the attorneys from the Utah Valley DA believed that these abuses were not criminal but contractual and civil in nature.
Parent (cherish wisdom), from her complaint to Utah authorities · Fornits forum · April 2004
I have been thrown on a concrete floor with a knee in my back crushing my lungs and someone's knee of my neck, someone sitting on my arms and then another sitting with their knees on my legs, just to make sure I wouldn't move when given a shot of Haldol, all because I just yelled out loud in my frustrations in the ob's room trying to get my anger out. I've been told by a staff that they know I'm claustrophobic and then was shoved by him into the ob's room and had the door shut on me.
Former student (darkhunterhope) · Fornits forum · May 2004
How can anyone justify shooting a child up with Thorazine, or sexually abusing a child, or denying a child medical attention. I've seen all these happen in my 11-month stay there in 1990-1991.
Former student · Petition comment, reposted on the Fornits forum · June 2004
