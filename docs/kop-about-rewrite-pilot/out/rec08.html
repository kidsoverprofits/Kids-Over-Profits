Rewrite

SLS Residential was a private residential mental health program for adolescents and young adults in Brewster, New York.[1][2] For five years it fought New York's Office of Mental Health (OMH) in hearings and courts.[3] In November 2006 the state fined it $110,000 and ordered it to stop admitting patients.[3] In 2007 former patients filed a federal class action alleging illegal restraints and isolation.[2] In August 2010 OMH revoked its licenses, and in November 2011 an appeals court upheld the revocation.[4]

SLS ran two community residences and an outpatient clinic.[3] OMH inspectors surveyed all three on November 6, 2006.[3] On November 17 the agency ordered SLS to stop admitting patients and stop using a building called "the Barn," and fined it $80,000 for eight violations.[3] On November 28 it added $30,000 for two breaches of those orders.[3] The violations included limits on patients' visits and phone calls, drug tests that required staff to watch patients, punishments handed out through a group called ADP, and staff hired without fingerprinting.[3] OMH also charged that staff held patients in manual restraints.[3] Its chairmen were Joseph Santoro and Alfred Bergman, and it did business through affiliated companies including SLS Health, SLS Wellness and Supervised Lifestyles Inc., headquartered on Route 6 in Southeast.[5] According to its website, as quoted in 2007, it charged as much as $950 a day.[6]

Also in 2007, two former patients, Nicholas J. Romano and Deborah A. Morgan, filed a federal class action on behalf of everyone SLS had treated.[2] They alleged illegal manual restraints, isolation rooms, nightly body and room searches, and denial of patients' rights to refuse treatment, to leave and to call their families.[2] They also claimed SLS discriminated against them under the Americans with Disabilities Act.[2] They sought $75 million in compensatory and $150 million in punitive damages.[2] Besides the companies and their chairmen, the suit named the employees Rob Giordano, Matt Sena and Gabe Caputo and the therapists Shawn Prichard and Robert Deletis.[6][5] The complaint alleged that SLS employees, "including but not limited to Sena and Caputo," grabbed and shoved patients, threw them to the ground, pinned them to the floor and sat on them, with the knowledge and approval of Santoro, Prichard, Bergman and Deletis.[6] In July 2008 the judge, Stephen Robinson, apologized to an SLS lawyer he had criticized over the lobbying of former patients to opt out of the suit.[7] He then found that SLS therapists had told about 80 former patients that their private medical records would be made public if they joined, some falsely saying the judge had ordered it.[2] Calling the conduct "astounding," he fined SLS $35,000, voided the opt-outs and held its top managers responsible.[2] SLS's chief clinical officer, Dr. Shawn Prichard, allegedly called the meeting where the plan was made.[2]

Meanwhile the state case moved against SLS.[8][7] In April 2008 a hearing officer upheld the 2006 findings, and that July OMH's commissioner adopted the fines.[8][7] On August 29, 2008, OMH moved to revoke SLS's operating certificates.[4] It charged that SLS kept using illegal restraints, kept violating patients' rights and lacked "the character and competence" to run a licensed program.[4] OMH also accused it of sedating patients who refused medication and of failing to report suicidal behavior and abuse complaints.[2] That same year the Commission on Accreditation of Rehabilitation Facilities (CARF) re-accredited SLS for three years.[9]

SLS took both actions to court.[7] In September 2008 co-owner Joseph Santoro said SLS had not had a fair hearing, and the company sued in state Supreme Court.[7] That December a Putnam County judge annulled the fines.[7][3] In November 2009 the Appellate Division reinstated $100,000 of them.[3] It struck only the $10,000 restraint fine, because OMH had never formally adopted its rule treating manual holds as restraints.[3] On August 9, 2010, OMH's commissioner revoked the certificates for both residences and the clinic.[4] The hearing officer had dismissed the restraint charges for the same reason but sustained every other charge.[4] On November 15, 2011, the Appellate Division confirmed the revocation and dismissed SLS's challenge on the merits.[4] In 2014 the court refused a request by outside parties to unseal the record of the fines case.[8]
