Rewrite

Straight – Cincinnati was the Ohio branch of Straight, Inc., the Florida-based drug program known for confrontational peer therapy.[1] It opened on January 6, 1982.[1] The Cincinnati Post gave its address as 6074 Branch Hill-Guinea Road in Milford.[2] Complaints of coercion began within weeks, and for more than two decades afterward Straight-descended programs operated in Milford, outside Cincinnati, drawing complaints of their own until the last of them shut down in 2008 or 2009.[1][3][4]

Criticism started in the branch's first year.[1] In mid-March 1982 the Cincinnati chapter of the American Civil Liberties Union filed complaints with the Ohio Bureau of Drug Abuse alleging "coercion and brainwashing" and asked the state to investigate.[1] That spring Cincinnati newspapers reported a teenager who said she had been forced into Straight, and in September a 14-year-old who was taken to the program by six people.[1] The state confirmed in May 1982 that it would investigate, and in June it cleared the program of abuse.[1]

Lawsuits followed.[5] From 1983 to 1985, according to a 2002 list of cases compiled by the Straight researcher Wes Fager, the Ohio attorney David J. Scacchetti filed suits against the branch complaining that children were beaten, painfully restrained, held in small isolation rooms for long periods, and denied food and sleep; two were settled out of court, and two were still pending in February 1991.[6] In 1986 Robert and Rosemary Weaver sued Straight for $1 million on behalf of their son, Eric.[5] The Cincinnati Post reported in 1987 that the suit was filed in June 1986 and said Eric's peers in the program would beat him and sit on his chest for over six hours.[2] A second $1 million suit followed in February 1987.[2] Straight officials denied that any client had been mentally or physically abused or wrongfully restrained.[2] At its peak, around 1985, the branch held more than 200 clients; by September 1987 it had about 65, and that month it announced it would end inpatient care after October 1 and see youths only for a few hours a day after school, blaming competition from hospital programs.[2]

The center soon closed altogether; the Enquirer later described it as closing in 1987 "amid rising conflict and falling funds," though an Enquirer columnist in 1989 put the closing in 1988 and blamed dwindling enrollment.[7][8] Fager's list says the Cincinnati attorney Richard Doyle led a three-year investigation of the branch, citing sensory deprivation, sexual confessions, children left sitting in their own excrement and children cutting themselves, and that Straight closed voluntarily on the day of trial.[6] The Weaver suit went to trial in Clermont County Common Pleas Court in May 1989.[5][9] Eric's parents, Robert and Rosemary Weaver, alleged that staff had assaulted him, isolated him and denied him adequate sleep and education during three months in 1985, and that Straight had misrepresented its program and its staff's qualifications.[5][9] The jury toured the empty building in Milford.[9] On the day the first witness, a former client and staff member, testified that force was routinely used, Straight offered a settlement, which the Weavers accepted; it was not an admission of wrongdoing, and the amount was not disclosed.[8] In October 1989 a federal judge approved a settlement of a class action brought by local donors over the $1 million property: half the proceeds went to Straight and the rest to the Greater Cincinnati Foundation for fees and local youth drug treatment programs.[7]

Kids Helping Kids, a program modeled on Straight, later ran a center in Milford.[3][4] It had started in Hebron, Kentucky, and moved into the former Straight building in 1993, the year it was incorporated in Ohio.[10][11] Its director was Penny Walker.[12] In November 2005 Cincinnati's WCPO Channel 9 aired a report on the program.[13] By 2007 its website called it "Kids Helping Kids, a Pathway Family Center," after Pathway, which was descended from Straight.[3][4] A January 2007 article reported that a mother had filed a child abuse complaint saying her 17-year-old son developed post-traumatic stress after being restrained and hit during therapy there, and that the survivor group ISAC had protested at the Milford center in 2006.[3]

The Milford center closed in the autumn of 2008; a Pathway spokeswoman told WCPO's I-Team that economic realities, not bad publicity or community pressure, closed it, and most of its teenagers were moved to Pathway centers in Indiana and Michigan.[14] Pathway's Ohio center had shut down by early 2009.[4] The journalist Maia Szalavitz reported then that Pathway Family Center "in Detroit and Ohio" had closed, and wrote that Pathway "was descended from the infamously abusive Straight Inc."[4]
