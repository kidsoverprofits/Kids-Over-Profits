Rewrite

Straight – Hampton Roads was a branch of Straight Inc., the Florida-based adolescent drug program, at the Greenbrier area of Chesapeake, Virginia. It opened in 1989 and closed on February 11, 1991, weeks after state licensing investigators arrived.[1][2][3] Investigators found that untrained people, including other clients, restrained teenagers who tried to leave, and that one teen's arm had been broken.[4] After it closed, former clients and parents described strip searches in front of other clients, painful restraints and 12-hour sessions of humiliation.[3]

Before 1989, Hampton Roads families sent their children to Straight's center in Springfield, Virginia. In May 1989, with about 70 local families in that program, the Daily Press reported that Straight planned to open in Chesapeake that summer.[1] In its own description to the paper, Straight was a five-phase program lasting about a year. New clients lived with "host families" whose own child was further along, in homes with alarm systems and locked knives and medicines, and visits from their own families had to be earned.[1] Treatment cost $11,000 to $12,000 a year.[1] The method relied on "positive peer pressure," giving older clients growing authority over newcomers.[4] By February 1991 the Chesapeake center had 58 clients from Hampton Roads and 24 staff, took no state or federal money, and was one of eight Straight centers nationwide.[2][4]

State regulators had already moved against Straight in Virginia. In 1990, after Washington-area parents complained about the Springfield center, a court agreement required both Virginia centers to change their restraint and living practices.[4] Early in 1991 parents' complaints brought a licensing specialist to the Chesapeake center, followed by a six-person team from the Department of Mental Health, Mental Retardation and Substance Abuse.[2] The licensing bureau found that "unqualified individuals, including clients, were allowed to restrain clients if they decided to leave the program," a practice assistant commissioner Jacqueline Ennis called dangerous; one teen's arm had been broken. It also told the program to limit how many clients each host home took and to check that the homes were adequate.[4] A Chesapeake mother said her 16-year-old son was thrown to the floor when he tried to run away, and another client grabbed his collar and burned his neck.[2] The week before, the bureau had refused to renew the Springfield center's license.[2]

In early February 1991 the center's administrator, Margaret Allen, announced it would close. She said investigators were "harassing" the program with unannounced visits, and Ennis answered that the inspections were "the normal course of the job."[2][4][5] The center closed on February 11, and all but two of its 58 clients were moved to other Straight programs.[3] Interviewed afterward, former clients described being held with arms and legs pulled back and heads forced to the floor, staff yelling so close that spit hit their faces, and strip searches in front of other clients. One said he was kept in 12-hour sessions and not allowed to use the bathroom for two hours. A Newport News mother said, "They did things that, if I had done them, I would have been charged with child abuse."[3] Straight denied the abuse allegations.[2]
