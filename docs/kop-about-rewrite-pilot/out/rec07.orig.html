Rewrite

The Family Foundation School was a boarding school for teenagers in Hancock, New York, founded by Tony and Betty Argiros.[1][2][2] It opened in 1977 by one account and settled on its Chapel Hill Road campus in Hancock in 1984.[1][1] The Argiros family owned and ran it throughout, and by 2011 the founders' children and grandchildren were in charge.[3][4][5] It was a founding member of NATSAP and stayed in the association from 1999 until 2014.[1][6] Students lived in four "family houses" overseen by staff called family leaders, and most stayed 18 months to two years or longer.[4][7][8][9][1]

Former students describe attack therapy, solitary confinement, physical and sexual abuse, and humiliation. Alumni said students were restrained by being wrapped in rugs and duct tape and shut in a janitor's closet. Students were also made to discipline one another, including chasing and restraining runaways. That practice was raised at congressional hearings of the Committee on Education and Labor in 2007 and 2008. On March 4, 2004, a 17-year-old student from Virginia fell from a second-story balcony at the school and died.[10] The student had disclosed suicidal thoughts earlier that day and spoken with a clinical social worker.[10] The death was ruled a suicide after a short investigation. In 2010 abuse allegations prompted an unannounced inspection by several New York state agencies. They reported no current abuse or neglect but remained concerned about former students' accounts.

The school was renamed Allynwood Academy in 2013 and closed in 2014.[11][1][12][13] By then the Family Foundation School Truth Campaign had gathered more than 100 alumni testimonials of abuse. One former student said in 2014 that he had been told to wake a student with a flood light, which damaged the student's eyes. A member of the Argiros family denied it happened. Rita Argiros, an administrator, said methods such as blanket restraints and forcing students to eat all their food had since been replaced.[5] In September 2018 a front-page New York Times article reported that at least 90 alumni had died since the closing, mostly by overdose or suicide.[13] One alumna counted 101 deaths.

Paul Geer taught choir at the school from 1991 until it closed and was also a family leader.[12][14][8] In April 2024 a federal indictment charged him with six counts of enticing three students to travel across state lines for sex.[15][16] He had taken them to Maine, Pennsylvania and Toronto in 1994, 2000 and 2001.[15] On March 3, 2025, an Albany jury convicted him on the four counts involving two of the students and deadlocked on the third.[15][17] The trial evidence showed he denied students food, forced them to eat regurgitated food, bound children in rugs and isolated them.[15][17] On September 4, 2025, he was sentenced to 27 years and three months in federal prison.[18][19] The court found that the punishments he imposed amounted to torture.[19]

In June 2026 a survivor sued, alleging physical and sexual abuse that included being made to dig their own grave and eat vomit.[20] The survivor claims local authorities ignored reports of abuse because of the owners' influence.[20] Liz Ianelli, held at the school from 1995 to 1998, took the name Survivor993 from the number of days she was confined there.[21] She told her story in the 2023 memoir "I See You, Survivor" and in the 2026 documentary "The Kids Are Not Alright."[21][22]
