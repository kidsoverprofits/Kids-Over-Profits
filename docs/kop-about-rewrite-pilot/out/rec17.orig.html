Now

Teen Challenge grew out of the Pentecostal preacher David Wilkerson's 1958 ministry to gang members in New York City, where he founded the first Teen Challenge center;[1] his 1963 book "The Cross and the Switchblade" told the story.[2] Now called Adult & Teen Challenge, it is a national Christian "discipleship" network affiliated with the Assemblies of God that runs residential programs for teenagers and adults,[3] with more than 200 centers in the United States and about 1,400 worldwide.[1] Its teen programs include boarding academies such as Columbus Girls Academy in Seale, Alabama, a 15-month Christian program for girls 13 to 17,[4] and a 15-month military-style boys' ranch in Bonifay, Florida.[5] Teen Challenge has argued that, as a ministry, it should not need treatment licensing,[3] and in 1995 the Texas Commission on Alcohol and Drug Abuse threatened to shut it down.[6]

According to Kids Over Profits research notes, Texas auditors had found violations at Teen Challenge's Texas program in 1995 and told it to meet health and safety standards to keep its license. Governor George W. Bush then intervened. In May 1996 he set up a Task Force on Faith-Based Programs to find regulatory barriers to religious providers, and in 1997 Texas passed House Bill 2481, which exempted faith-based drug treatment programs from state licensing.[7] The notes also record that Henry Lozano, a Teen Challenge director in California, later served as a deputy assistant to President Bush.[7]

Naomi Wood, 17, died after a seizure in May 2020 at Lakeland Girls Academy in Florida, about three and a half months after she arrived; she had asked for medical help for stomach pain, Florida's Department of Children and Families found inadequate supervision and medical neglect, and the academy closed in March 2022.[8] Woodbury Reports had listed the Lakeland program as a division of Teen Challenge.[9] A 2021 New Yorker investigation reported that parents enrolling children in Teen Challenge programs signed contracts waiving the right to sue.[10]

In April 2026 nine women sued The Refuge Girls Academy in Lebanon, Indiana and its operator, Central Indiana Teen Challenge, in federal court, alleging humiliating discipline and forced labor;[11] by June the suit had 35 plaintiffs.[12] The academy closed on September 10, 2026.[13] Its lawyer said running it was "no longer tenable" while defending the suit, and Indiana Teen Challenge denies most of the allegations.[2] In Oklahoma, former students of New Lifehouse Academy, run by Adult & Teen Challenge of Oklahoma, described restraint, food restriction and unpaid labor to The Frontier; the program is not licensed to provide mental health treatment[14] and faces federal lawsuits filed in 2026.[15] Former students also sued Kansas City Girls Academy.[16] Teen Challenge continues to operate.

Rewrite

Teen Challenge, now called Adult & Teen Challenge, is a Pentecostal ministry that runs long-term Christian residential programs for teenagers and adults.[1][2] It has called itself "The Faith Based Solution to the Drug Epidemic".[1] Since the mid-1990s it has fought to keep its programs out of state licensing, and its fight in Texas became a model for George W. Bush's faith-based policy, first as governor and then as president.[3][4][5] Former residents have described abuse in its programs for just as long. A boy sued its Dallas boys' ranch in 1998.[6] By 2012 Florida child-welfare investigators had been called to its Panhandle boys' academy 24 times.[7] In 2020 a 17-year-old girl died at its girls' academy in Lakeland, Florida, weeks after the academy refused her requests to see a doctor.[8] When families sue, Teen Challenge points to the waivers and Christian arbitration agreements they signed.[9][10]

The Pentecostal preacher David Wilkerson founded the first Teen Challenge center in New York City in 1958, as a ministry to the city's gang members.[11][1] His 1963 bestseller "The Cross and the Switchblade" is his own account of that work.[10] The ministry now operates under Assemblies of God U.S. Missions from headquarters in Ozark, Missouri, and claims more than 200 centers in the United States and about 1,400 worldwide.[2][11] Each center is a separate nonprofit. In 2016 each paid headquarters $100 a month for the name, the curriculum and staff training.[2]

For teenagers, Teen Challenge runs Christian boarding academies with 15-month programs; in 2016 families paid about $1,500 a month.[12][2] Its boys' academy in Bonifay, in the Florida Panhandle, has operated since the late 1990s and is also called Gateway Christian Military Academy.[7] In 2007 it took boys aged 12 to 17, called them cadets and put them through farm chores and military drill. It admitted boys with ADHD or mild depression but gave no psychiatric medication.[1] Its girls' academies have included Lakeland Girls Academy in Florida, Kansas City Girls Academy in Missouri, The Refuge in Lebanon, Indiana, and New Lifehouse Academy in Oklahoma.[8][13][14][15]

The licensing fight that made Teen Challenge a national political cause began in Texas. In 1995 the Texas Commission on Alcohol and Drug Abuse gave Teen Challenge of South Texas a 49-page list of violations.[3] Inspectors found "No qualified credential counselors on staff", an inoperable smoke alarm system, and handling of medications that was "badly out of compliance".[16] The commission told the program to meet the state's health and safety standards or lose its license.[17] Governor George W. Bush took the ministry's side. "Teen Challenge should view itself as a pioneer in how Texas approaches faith-based programs," he said that year.[16] In May 1996 he created a Task Force on Faith-Based Programs to find regulatory obstacles to religious providers, and the director of Teen Challenge of South Texas sat on it.[17][3] The next year House Bill 2481 let faith-based drug treatment programs register with the state instead of being licensed, as long as they did not treat minors.[18][17] By 2002 Teen Challenge ran five drug treatment centers in Texas, none of them licensed.[6] When Bush ran for president in 2000, he held up Teen Challenge as a prototype and promised to do nationally what he had done in Texas.[5]

As president, Bush kept shielding the ministry. Teen Challenge centers in other states also refused licensing, arguing that it would force a "disease model" that "denies addiction is a manifestation of sin".[4] In 2005 federal field offices cut off food stamps to residents of unlicensed Teen Challenge centers in Massachusetts and Texas. Residents typically handed their food stamps to staff, who pooled them to feed the program. That August the Bush administration reversed the cutoff in a letter to all 50 governors.[4] In September 2007 Bush named Henry Lozano, who had directed Teen Challenge in California, a Deputy Assistant to the President.[19][20]

Florida let children's homes that claimed a religious exemption answer to a private Christian association, the Florida Association of Christian Child Caring Agencies, instead of the state.[7] Teen Challenge's Florida academies worked under that system, and its record there shows what the exemption allowed.[8][7] In 2008 staff at the Bonifay academy shaved the head of 15-year-old Samson Lehman and kept making him exercise after he collapsed again and again. They waited a full day to take him to a hospital. By then his organs were shutting down, and he needed months of dialysis. The Department of Children and Families verified medical neglect, but the local state attorney filed no charges.[7] A 2012 Tampa Bay Times investigation found that the academy was one of seven religious homes that accounted for two-thirds of Florida's abuse hotline complaints against such homes over the previous decade.[7]

Naomi Wood, 17, arrived at Lakeland Girls Academy from Vermont on February 4, 2020.[21] In April she asked to see a doctor for chronic stomach pain. The academy refused, never consulted a physician and gave her Pepto-Bismol about 20 times. She vomited for two days and was found unresponsive in her room on May 19, 2020. The medical examiner listed a seizure disorder.[8] The Department of Children and Families found that "inadequate supervision and medical neglect" contributed to her death, and that the academy used shunning as punishment. Teen Challenge's regional CEO told investigators the directors had done a "phenomenal job".[8] No criminal charges were filed, and the academy closed in March 2022.[21][9] In March 2024, at Treasure Coast Boys Academy in Vero Beach, a staff member was arrested for allegedly molesting a 12-year-old student. A civil suit alleges the school knew of his behavior and kept him on staff.[22]

Lawsuits against Teen Challenge go back decades, and the ministry has often kept them out of court. In 1998 a boy sued the Dallas Teen Challenge Boys Ranch and the Assemblies of God, alleging that a counselor who was a convicted drug trafficker had molested him and two other boys.[6] In 2013 a Florida appeals court held the mother of Nicklaus Ellison, who died of an overdose, to the Christian arbitration agreement he had signed when he enrolled at 19.[23] Naomi Wood's parents sued Teen Challenge of Florida and Adult & Teen Challenge USA in April 2022.[8] In November 2023 a judge ruled they could not sue in court, because before enrolling her they had signed a Christian Conciliation and Arbitration Agreement. The case went to Christian arbitration, under Biblical principles and before a Christian mediator instead of a judge.[9]

Since late 2025, women who were sent to its girls' academies as teenagers have brought a new wave of suits, many alleging forced labor. In December 2025 two women sued Kansas City Girls Academy in federal court under the federal trafficking law, alleging that from 2009 to 2011 they worked unpaid at least four hours each weekday.[13] In July 2026 three more former residents sued in state court, alleging evaluations that became strip searches, cameras watching bedrooms and delayed medical care.[24] On April 8, 2026, nine women sued Indiana Teen Challenge over its girls' program in Lebanon, and by June the suit had 35 plaintiffs describing abuse from 2011 to 2024.[14] They allege girls were kept in a "safe room" for up to 30 days without human contact. One says she was forbidden to speak for nine months.[14] Another says she was made to cut grass on her hands and knees with children's safety scissors.[10] Teen Challenge denies almost all of it, though it admits residents were sometimes forbidden to talk, which it calls a "behavioral intervention". Its motion to dismiss cites church-state protections, statutes of limitations and signed waivers.[10] The program, by then called The Refuge Girls Academy, closed on September 10, 2026.[25] Its lawyer said running it was "no longer tenable" given the cost of the defense and of insurance.[14]

In Oklahoma, The Frontier interviewed 24 former students of New Lifehouse Academy, run by Adult & Teen Challenge of Oklahoma. Thirteen who attended between 2012 and 2024 described the same staff member restraining girls. Students also described food restriction and unpaid work at a summer fireworks stand. The school is not licensed to provide mental health treatment. Two former students filed federal forced-labor suits in summer 2026. Teen Challenge said it uses restraint only when a student may pose an immediate risk of harm.[15] Its adult programs face the same labor claims: in September 2026 Massachusetts fined Adult & Teen Challenge, Northeast $1.15 million after more than 200 men in its programs worked for private businesses without pay from 2017 to 2025. Its CEO said the nonprofit "strongly disagrees" and would contest the citations.[26] From the Texas inspectors in 1995 to the Indiana lawsuit in 2026, Teen Challenge has answered its critics the same way: its faith, it argues, puts it beyond outside rules.[16][4][10]
