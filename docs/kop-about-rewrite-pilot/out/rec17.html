Rewrite

Teen Challenge, now called Adult & Teen Challenge, is a Pentecostal ministry that runs long-term Christian residential programs for teenagers and adults.[1][2] It has called itself "The Faith Based Solution to the Drug Epidemic".[1] An addiction historian has credited it with "developing the foundations of the troubled-teen industry".[3] Since 1995 it has fought to keep its programs out of state licensing, and its fight in Texas became a model for George W. Bush's faith-based policy, first as governor and then as president.[4][5][3] Former residents have described abuse in its programs for just as long.[6] A boy sued its Dallas boys' ranch in 1998.[6] The New Yorker reported in 2021 that, from 2001, girls at its Lakeland, Florida, academy had been pressured to give up their babies for adoption.[3] By 2012 Florida child-welfare investigators had been called to its Panhandle boys' academy 24 times.[7] In 2020 a 17-year-old girl died at the Lakeland academy after it refused her requests to see a doctor.[8] When families sue, Teen Challenge points to the waivers and Christian arbitration agreements they signed.[3][9][10]

David Wilkerson, a Pentecostal preacher from rural Pennsylvania, began preaching to teenage gang members in New York in 1958 and opened the first Teen Challenge center in Brooklyn in 1960.[3][11] His 1963 bestseller "The Cross and the Switchblade" is his own account of that work, and students in Teen Challenge programs are encouraged to read it.[3] Wilkerson helped pastors open centers across the country; in 1972 he told The New York Times, "It's almost like a franchise."[3] Conservative politicians embraced the ministry.[3] Ronald Reagan said the program "works," and in 1984 Nancy Reagan visited a center in Tennessee for her "Just Say No" campaign.[3] Teen Challenge now operates under Assemblies of God U.S. Missions from headquarters in Ozark, Missouri, and claims more than 200 centers in the United States and about 1,400 worldwide.[2][11] Each center is a separate nonprofit.[2] In 2016 each paid headquarters $100 a month for the name, the curriculum and staff training.[2]

For teenagers, Teen Challenge runs Christian boarding academies with 15-month programs; in 2016 families paid about $1,500 a month.[12][2] One of them, Columbus Girls Academy in Seale, Alabama, ran a 15-month Christian program for girls aged 13 to 17 in 2012.[12] Many of the teens are not addicted to drugs; some are sent for depression, sexual activity, being gay or ordinary rebellion.[3] Its boys' academy in Bonifay, in the Florida Panhandle, has operated since the late 1990s and is also called Gateway Christian Military Academy.[7] In 2007 it took boys aged 12 to 17, called them cadets and put them through farm chores and military drill.[1] It admitted boys with ADHD or mild depression but gave no psychiatric medication.[1] Its girls' academies have included Lakeland Girls Academy in Florida, Kansas City Girls Academy in Missouri, a program in Lebanon, Indiana, and New Lifehouse Academy in Oklahoma.[3][13][14][15] At Lakeland and other centers, former students described strict rules.[3] At Lakeland, new girls spent six weeks as "Little Sisters," barred from speaking except to two older students.[3] Punishments included copying a Scripture-based paragraph up to 150 times and "Silence," a ban on all communication with other students.[3] Pairs of students put on "Relationship Restriction" had to act as if the other were dead.[3] Weekly 15-minute phone calls home were monitored, and staff ended calls that strayed into forbidden subjects.[3]

The licensing fight that made Teen Challenge a national political cause began in Texas.[4][3] In 1995 the Texas Commission on Alcohol and Drug Abuse gave Teen Challenge of South Texas, in San Antonio, a 49-page list of violations.[4][3] Inspectors found "No qualified credential counselors on staff", an inoperable smoke alarm system, and handling of medications that was "badly out of compliance".[16] The commission told the program to meet the state's health and safety standards or lose its license.[17] Governor George W. Bush took the ministry's side.[18] "Teen Challenge is going to exist," he said, "and licensing standards have to be different from what they are today."[18] In May 1996 he created a Task Force on Faith-Based Programs to find regulatory obstacles to religious providers, and the director of Teen Challenge of South Texas sat on it.[17][4] In 1997 Texas passed two bills that came out of the task force.[19][17] House Bill 2481 let faith-based drug treatment programs register with the state instead of being licensed, as long as they did not treat minors.[19][17] House Bill 2482 let faith-based children's homes be accredited by a private Christian association instead of being licensed by the state.[18] Under that system the rate of confirmed abuse and neglect at the eight homes it accredited was 25 percent, against less than 1 percent at state-licensed homes, and in 2001 the Legislature let it lapse.[18] By 2002 Teen Challenge ran five drug treatment centers in Texas, none of them licensed.[6] When Bush ran for president in 2000, he held up Teen Challenge as a prototype and promised to do nationally what he had done in Texas.[5]

As president, Bush kept shielding the ministry.[20] Teen Challenge centers in other states also refused licensing, arguing that it would force a "disease model" that "denies addiction is a manifestation of sin".[20] In 2005 federal field offices cut off food stamps to residents of unlicensed Teen Challenge centers in Massachusetts and Texas.[20] Residents typically handed their food stamps to staff, who pooled them to feed the program.[20] That August the Bush administration reversed the cutoff in a letter to all 50 governors.[20] His administration also made $100 million available for faith-based drug treatment, announcing in 2004 that people seeking treatment could now choose "programs like Teen Challenge."[3] In September 2007 Bush named Henry Lozano, who had directed Teen Challenge in California for about a decade, a Deputy Assistant to the President.[21][3]

Florida has let religious children's homes avoid state licensing since 1984.[3][7] Homes that claim the exemption answer instead to a private body, the Florida Association of Christian Child Caring Agencies, and the Department of Children and Families can step in only when abuse or neglect is alleged.[3][7] Teen Challenge's Florida academies worked under that system.[7] In 2008 staff at the Bonifay academy shaved the head of 15-year-old Samson Lehman and kept making him exercise after he collapsed again and again.[7] They waited a full day to take him to a hospital.[7] By then his organs were shutting down, and he needed months of dialysis.[7] The department verified medical neglect, but the local state attorney filed no charges.[7] A 2012 Tampa Bay Times investigation found that the academy was one of seven religious homes that accounted for two-thirds of Florida's abuse hotline complaints against such homes over the previous decade.[7] By 2021 the department had investigated the Lakeland academy five times in 13 years, over allegations including "bizarre punishment" and "physical injury, asphyxiation."[3] In March 2024 a staff member at Treasure Coast Boys Academy in Vero Beach was arrested for allegedly molesting a 12-year-old student.[22] A civil suit alleges the school knew of his behavior and kept him on staff.[22] Outside Florida, reviewers have posted accounts of Teen Challenge's Prayer Mountain Boys Academy in Georgia, later called Pine Mountain Boys Academy, since 2020 (see Survivor testimony below).[23]

At the Lakeland academy, The New Yorker reported, girls were pressured to give up their babies.[3] In 2001 Teen Challenge took over a building there that had housed Help Unfortunate Girls, a home for unmarried pregnant women.[3] Deanna Doucette, the first pregnant girl to arrive after the takeover, came that year at 14.[3] Weeks before her due date she ran away; she told the police who brought her back, "Don't take me back—they're forcing me to give away my baby."[3] Five months were added to her stay for running away.[3] Amber Foster, 17, sent there by a court, tried to withdraw her consent to an adoption but could not mail the form in time.[3] "My child was stolen from me," she told the magazine.[3] In 2011 Emma Burris, 15, learned she was pregnant soon after arriving.[3] When she asked about an abortion, she said, a counselor "looked shocked, and she said, 'That's not an option.'"[3] The directors, Greg and Essie Del Valle, worked out an adoption plan with her parents, who later refused to sign a transfer to a program that would have let her keep her son.[3] Soon after she gave birth and returned, the directors told her she needed to start the program over again.[3] A lawyer for Teen Challenge said "the program has no records" of Emma's being forced to give up her child, and that Teen Challenge "does not provide counsel regarding adoption."[3]

Naomi Wood, 17, arrived at Lakeland Girls Academy from Vermont on February 4, 2020.[24] In April she asked to see a doctor for chronic stomach pain.[8] The academy refused, never consulted a physician and gave her Pepto-Bismol about 20 times during her stay.[8] On the evening of May 18 her symptoms worsened, and she vomited repeatedly through the night and the next day.[8] On May 19, 2020, she was found unresponsive in her dorm room.[8] The Polk County medical examiner listed a seizure disorder.[8] A Department of Children and Families report concluded that her death was due in part to inadequate supervision and medical neglect by the academy, and called her case "consistent with delay in seeking care and medical neglect."[8][3][25] The report's author, Dr. Carol Lilly, also found that the academy used shunning, forbidding girls to speak to one another, as punishment.[8][25] The sheriff's office brought no charges.[8][3] Teen Challenge's Southeast regional president and CEO, Brice Maddock, told investigators the directors had done a "phenomenal job".[8] The academy closed in March 2022.[24][9]

Lawsuits against Teen Challenge go back decades, and the ministry has often kept them out of court.[6] In 1998 a boy sued the Dallas Teen Challenge Boys Ranch and the Assemblies of God, alleging that a counselor who was a convicted drug trafficker had molested him and two other boys.[6] In 2006 Teen Challenge Prayer Mountain Boys Academy sued Pike County, Georgia, in federal court.[26] Residents and parents sign contracts waiving the right to sue in civil court and agreeing to "Biblically based mediation" instead; parents also agree "not to interfere with the custody or management" of their child.[3] In October 2013 a Florida appeals court ruled in Spivey v. Teen Challenge that Pamela Spivey, suing over the death of her son, Nicklaus Ellison, was bound by the Christian conciliation agreement he had signed when he enrolled at 19 in its year-long substance abuse program.[27] In 2021 a former student, Amaya Rasheed, sued Teen Challenge of Oklahoma, alleging that she was "physically restrained against her will" until she couldn't breathe, and was denied medical care.[3] Naomi Wood's parents sued Teen Challenge of Florida and Adult & Teen Challenge USA in April 2022.[8] In November 2023 a judge ruled they could not sue in court, because before enrolling her they had signed a Christian Conciliation and Arbitration Agreement.[9] The case went to Christian arbitration, under Biblical principles and before a Christian mediator instead of a judge.[9]

Since late 2025, women who were sent to its girls' academies as teenagers have brought a new wave of suits, many alleging forced labor.[3] Some of the abuse they describe had been reported years earlier.[3] In 2021 The New Yorker quoted the journal of Megan, a student sent to the Lebanon, Indiana, program in 2020. "Since I've been here," she wrote, "I've witnessed 13 suicide attempts not including my own."[3] At Kansas City, the magazine reported, students were given self-improvement "projects," such as carrying a backpack of rocks for several days or wearing a weighted belt.[3] In December 2025 two women sued Kansas City Girls Academy in federal court under the federal trafficking law, alleging that from 2009 to 2011 they worked unpaid at least four hours each weekday.[13] In July 2026 three more former residents sued in state court, alleging evaluations that became strip searches, cameras watching bedrooms and delayed medical care.[28] On April 8, 2026, nine women sued Indiana Teen Challenge over its Lebanon girls' program, and by June the suit had 35 plaintiffs describing abuse from 2011 to 2024.[14] Fox59 reported that the complaint alleges girls were made to work unpaid, including at Butler and Purdue.[29] They allege girls were kept in a "safe room" for up to 30 days without human contact.[14] One says she was forbidden to speak for nine months.[14] Another says she was made to cut grass on her hands and knees with children's safety scissors.[10] Teen Challenge denies almost all of it, though it admits residents were sometimes forbidden to talk, which it calls a "behavioral intervention".[10] Its motion to dismiss cites church-state protections, statutes of limitations and signed waivers.[10] The program, by then called The Refuge Girls Academy, closed on September 10, 2026.[30] Its lawyer said running it was "no longer tenable" given the cost of the defense and of insurance.[14]

In Oklahoma, The Frontier interviewed 24 former students of New Lifehouse Academy, run by Adult & Teen Challenge of Oklahoma.[15] Thirteen who attended between 2012 and 2024 described the same staff member restraining girls.[15] Students also described food restriction and unpaid work at a summer fireworks stand.[15] The school is not licensed to provide mental health treatment.[15] Two former students filed federal forced-labor suits in summer 2026.[15] Teen Challenge said it uses restraint only when a student may pose an immediate risk of harm.[15] Its adult programs face the same labor claims.[3] Adult residents often work at least 40 hours a week without pay, which the organization calls job training; a former director of its Jacksonville men's center called it "labor trafficking."[3] In September 2026 Massachusetts fined Adult & Teen Challenge, Northeast $1.15 million after more than 200 men in its programs worked for private businesses without pay from 2017 to 2025.[31] Its CEO said the nonprofit "strongly disagrees" and would contest the citations.[31] From the Texas inspectors in 1995 to the Indiana lawsuit in 2026, Teen Challenge has answered its critics the same way: its faith, it argues, puts it beyond outside rules.[16][20][10]
