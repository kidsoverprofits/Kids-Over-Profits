Welcome to the Academy at Swift River\!

Welcome to the Academy at Swift River\! We are please to have you and your child with us, and we look forward to working with you as your child progresses through the ASR program.

We understand that enrolling your child at ASR may have been a difficult decision. Life has, perhaps, been challenging in recent months or years. You may have feared for your child’s heath and well-being, and even his or her life. It is not easy to make important decisions at such times. You have shown courage and determination by searching for help, and we salute you.

As you have been worried and wondered, explored options, and tried to determine a course of action that would be best for your child, you have possibly been aware of your own underlying pain. It is hard to face the fall of our dreams for our children. No doubt you expected ups and downs along the rocky road of adolescence, but likely you also anticipated the joy of sharing all the usual activities and joys of your child’s teen years as well- sports and other school teams, clubs and special activities, the senior prom. It must appear now that many of those dreams are not to be.

But it is time for a new dream- that of helping your child rediscover the true qualities that make him or her a unique and wonderful individual, reclaim the inherent goodness and promise within, and redirect all that amazing adolescent energy into positive, life- affirming directions. You have taken the first steps towards the realization of that new dream in finding help for yourself, your child, and your family.

You and your child will grow together as you progress through the ASR program, and it is our privilege to facilitate and be a part of your work. Thank you for sharing your child with us; such trust is a high honor indeed. We pledge to maintain integrity, respect, and compassion as we work together toward achieving positive outcomes for you and your family during your child’s stay at ASR.

Let us begin\!

ASR Mission Statement

To help underachieving teens get back on track by restoring relationships, rediscovering academic success, and preparing them to meet the challenges of adolescence and young adulthood. ASR Philosophy

We believe in the innate goodness and potential of the young people with whom we work and in their ability to rise to the challenges of self-discovery. We believe that their growth is best promoted by encouraging, guiding, and supporting them as they engage in the developmental tasks of adolescence. We believe that self-esteem is grounded by the establishment and refinement of competencies which reflect the unique gifts of each individual and encompass the practical skills essential for living in a complex, often demanding, and increasingly diverse society.

We share with them the adventure of life-long learning, and respect for the dignity, integrity, and uniqueness of the individual. We believe that teaching by example is not merely the best way, but the only way, to show adolescents how to embrace responsibility for their lives as members of the human community. We realize that the balance and integrity which we strive to achieve among the emotional, intellectual, physical, and spiritual elements of our students’ lives must also be manifest in our own lives.

ASR Slang- Glossary of Terms

AAB- Arts and Activities Building. This is the building with the weight room and basketball court.

AA/ NA Meetings- Alcoholics Anonymous/ Narcotics Anonymous. ASR is dedicated to helping adolescents with their substance abuse. AA/NA meetings are an important part of our program. Regular meetings are held both on campus and off campus on a weekly basis.

Academic Term- The academic year is divided into Terms of ten weeks each. Each course carries 1/3 academic credit; thus three terms are required to take a whole year’s course for 1.0 academic credit. Elective courses tend to be offered only for one term. Full courses will be offered for three consecutive terms. Note that the academic term is less than the calendar year.

ACD- Alternative Curriculum Day- These are short breaks from the normal academic schedule that occur periodically throughout the year, usually at mid-terms. The focus and/or activities vary and may include day or overnight trips. ACDs are designed to offer our students a break from classes and provide fun and unconventional learning experiences. During week-long school breaks, we have Family Resolution Weeks instead of ACDs.

Agreements- These are the rules of the school, which include our three major agreements- no sex, no drugs, no violence. When students are following the rules they are considered “in agreement”. When they are struggling with following the rules, they are “out of agreement”.

Ambassadors- This is a peer-mentoring program that is run by the Admissions Department. Students who are doing well with their program can become Ambassadors. Their responsibilities include giving families tours, answering questions from a student’s perspective, and helping new students get settled into ASR upon enrollment.

Appointment time- Students can speak with a staff or another student by scheduling an appointment. Appointment time is a nice quiet time to talk with one other person. It is usually held in the evening before bedtime.

Bans- At some point in the program, most kids will go on bans with another student or activity. Bans mean that students can have no communication with whomever they are on bans with- this includes talking, eye contact, note passing, etc. This allows us to break up inappropriate relationships, negative friendships, and cliques. Students may be placed on bans with certain activities if they have abused a privilege or if they are neglecting other important obligations.

5

Certificate of Completion- Each student who completes the ASR program satisfactorily receives a Certificate of Completion.

Consequence- Every action comes with a consequence, be it negative or positive. It is important that we hold a firm line with kids in order to provide safety and health. There are various negative consequences for infractions of agreements and also positive consequences to give out when students are doing well.

Cover-Ups- Ways of denying or hiding the truth from ourselves are called “cover-ups”. Students may use anger as a cover-up, lashing out at others rather than admitting that they are experiencing emotional pain beneath the anger. Or they may adopt a victim mentality, blaming others or circumstances for whatever is wrong rather than accepting responsibility.

Crews and Crew Leaders- Each morning, students split up into crews who clean different areas of our campus. Crew Leaders are students who organize the work crews and make sure their teams are working hard.

Dorm Heads- Each dorm room has a student who is in charge of the room, called a Dorm Head. They make sure each of their roommates helps clean and be on time to meetings. They also mediate conflicts and communicate any difficulties to staff.

Floor Coordinator/ Person Running the Floor- Each evening, there is one staff who is in charge of the school and organizing staff responsibilities. This person is usually a Residential Supervisor. This person tells the Mentors what the tasks are for the evening and is the person who handles any challenges of the evening.

Great Room- This is the room on the second floor of the Main Building where general meetings and activities take place. Movies, groups, and closings occur here.

Group- Group happens several times a week and is a time to process issues about life and relationships. Sometimes groups will be with teams, with a mixed group of students, or about a particular subject (eating disorders, cutting, etc). When there is a mixed group, students and staff can request to be in group with people that they need to speak with.

Growth Book- All enrolling students receive and work on the Growth Book while in Pathways. The Growth Book contains reading and writing assignments, behavioral expectations, social guidelines, etc.

HO\!- A Native American term, used to signify enthusiastic agreement with or approval of what another is saying. You will often hear this in meetings and in groups.

Individual Achievement Plan- When a student enrolls at ASR, a treatment team is immediately formed to work with the student and his/her family. This includes therapists, residential life staff, teachers, and the parents of the student. This team will

6

work together to create an individualized treatment plan that suits each child’s therapeutic and academic needs and the IAP will be used (and may evolve) throughout the program.

Lifesteps- There are five Lifesteps at ASR. These are personal growth workshops that occur at specific points in the program. They are designed to help students get in touch with some feelings and learn new ideas that will stay with them throughout their lives.

Meetings- Meetings occur all the time here. There are meetings before breakfast and dinner and at the beginnings of groups and special activities. They are opportunities to do head counts, make announcements, and share stories. Each night we do a special closing meeting where students and staff may talk to the whole school. They share funny stories, emotional parts of their lives, and ask for help. These closings are very sacred.

Mentors- The Residential Life Department consists of supervisors and mentors who are with the students during afternoons, evenings, and weekends. Mentors run sports, study halls, trips, and other activities. There are also overnight mentors who supervise the students while they are sleeping.

Parent Communicators (PCs)- Each student has a clinician who is assigned to work specifically with him/her and their family. PCs make phone calls to families on a weekly basis.

Pathways- During their transition to our school, students begin their work in our Pathways program. Pathways students have additional groups to work on behavior, social, and communication guidelines, anxiety over being a new student at a new school, and group dynamics. Adventure learning curriculum is the trademark of this part of the program and may include hiking and ropes course work.

Resolutions- Family Resolutions happen for families several times during the course of our program. During Resolutions, families come to campus for group therapy with their children. Group therapy is followed by a visit- some visits are on campus, some are at hotels, and some are at the children’s homes.

Restrictions- When a student is out of agreement, he may need extra help to get back on track. Restrictions are folders with writing assignments that are designed to help kids work through particular issues and struggles. When students are on restriction, the activities they can participate are limited so that their attention stays on their emotional and behavioral work. There are also academic restrictions that provide kids with additional structured time and support when they are falling behind in classes.

School Store- The school store is located in the dining room and sells snacks, hygiene products, and school supplies. Each student earns money for school store each week and may earn additional money if they are in leadership positions or have earned positive consequences. Hygiene supplies are available for students to buy regardless of if they have earned the privilege of purchasing snacks.

Student Services Team- The SST meets weekly to review students who are having particular difficulties with any aspect of the ASR program. Discussion may result in specific recommendations for changes in approach or other action, as indicated by the nature of the student’s difficulty and at the consensus of the SST members. This team includes clinicians, residential life staff, teachers, psychiatrists, and health services. This multi-disciplinary approach helps ensure that nothing is overlooked and that every possible approach within the scope of our program is employed to meet our student’s needs.

Study Hall- On weekday evenings we have study hall. Mentors supervise these silent study sessions and make sure all kids are working and getting the help they need.

Teams- All students at ASR are divided into five teams. The teams are Mustangs, Wolves, Buffalo, Cougars, and Eagles. Students are assigned to a team upon enrollment and stay with the same team for the duration of their stay at ASR. Each team has several clinicians, supervisors, teachers, and mentors assigned to it to specifically work with those students. The team model provides us with groups that resemble families and support peer mentorship and positive culture.

Team Reflection Time- Team Reflections are held several times a week after study hall. Students break up into their teams and meet with their team mentors for approximated 45 minutes. Team reflection time is a time to wrap up the day in a calm and thoughtful way that helps the team bond. Activities may include yoga or mediation, quiet games, celebrations of team accomplishments, and support for team members who have had a difficult day. Activities should help the students to transition to a peaceful closing meeting and bedtime.

Upper School and Lower School- When kids successfully complete the Pathways program, they enter Lower School. Students stay in Lower School until they have completed their second Family Resolution and are accepted into Upper School. Upper School kids earn various leadership opportunities, trips, and privileges.

War-story- This term is used as a verb and it means to talk with other students about negative behaviors from the past (drug use, sexual activity, etc) in a way that glorifies the action. War-storying is not permitted at ASR.

Weekend Direction- On each Friday, sheets are displayed in the main building that tell students what privileges and consequences they have earned for over the weekend. Staff provides consequences to Residential Supervisors who ensure that they happen over the weekend.

Work Projects- Each weekend, there are service projects that are consequences for negative behavior. They are supervised by mentors and may involve shoveling, gardening, cleaning buildings, etc. Work projects are community service opportunities to teach students about giving back to the community when their previous behavior has had a negative impact.

ASR in a Nutshell

The Academy at Swift River, a program of Aspen Education Group (www.aspeneducation.com), is defining what a therapeutic boarding school in the year 2006 should be. As a “clinical boarding school”, and one that is at the forefront of the private, therapeutic education industry with regard to evolving from a traditional “emotional growth” school to one that offers an individualized approach to college preparatory academics, counseling, and healthy life style activities based on specific student and family needs, ASR is a unique program that deserves a second look.

The Academy at Swift River is a therapeutic boarding school that embodies New England traditions and provides a robust college preparatory experience supplemented by strong therapeutic services. Our continuum is built upon the mastery of the developmental tasks of adolescence and is being enhanced by incorporating the latest evidenced based practices and focused academics. Although our program has been in existence since 1997, we are constantly evolving and improving our services based on the needs of our students and families as well as referral sources. Our current direction and program matriculation includes the development of a stronger, more individualized approach that is relational in nature rather than confrontational, allowing the student to internalize change versus superficially demonstrating compliance. We do this through a cognitive behavioral and psychodynamic approach that is best suited to serve a student with a strong reflective capacity. A ‘typical’ ASR student is one with a higher than average IQ (95 full scale minimum), having academic difficulties (mild learning differences), having family difficulties (parent-child relational, oppositional, divorce, etc.) and has engaged is substance use and abuse.

Our individualized academic program focuses on a traditional curriculum. We teach critical thinking and self-advocacy in order to develop confident and committed learners. We have low student to teacher ratios and a means to provide individual help and to develop specific learning strategies for students who need a little extra support due to a mild learning difference. Currently, we are a candidate for NEACS accreditation and are part of the local Mohawk Regional School District.

At ASR we strive to offer a memorable and challenging high school experience, which draws from the best of traditional college preparatory curricula, combined with innovative, individualized approaches to learning. Students come to ASR with varying levels of success in their educational careers: Some have maintained consistently high grades; others have not performed to their fullest potential in a number of years. Regardless, students leave ASR with a clear sense of achievement, and an understanding of who they are as learners.

Our curriculum is based on the Massachusetts Frameworks, ensuring a liberal arts course of study in the major content areas of mathematics, natural sciences, humanities and the visual arts. The core curriculum is combined with electives from these areas, as

well as extra-curricular opportunities for athletics, community service learning, and the performing arts. Classes are taught in 10-week trimesters, which continue throughout the calendar year. The result is a well-rounded, rigorous education, which prepares students for advanced college study and beyond. We are proud to be able to state that 100% of our students who graduate high school from ASR are able to pursue higher education at the college or university of their choice.

At ASR, teachers, counselors and students collaborate to develop a plan which helps set the course for successful educational and therapeutic experiences. The Individual Achievement Plan helps all who work with a student identify, understand and expand on student strengths, while targeting areas in which a student needs further development. Teachers augment this process by helping students understand their unique learning styles and personality types, and how these influence academic success. Our classes are small, with a 1:8 average teacher-student ratio. This allows teachers to tailor the learning environment in order to make material accessible to all students. Our teachers have a keen awareness of learning differences, and most have been trained to use neurodevelopment profiles (from Dr. Mel Levine’s internationally recognized Schools Attuned program) to optimize student success. Our Learning Needs Specialist works with students, teachers and clinicians to ensure that each student’s individual needs are met, allowing for integration between students’ academic and therapeutic work.

ASR offers a host of supplemental services to guide students in their education, such as Princeton Review SAT / ACT test preparation, and college- or boarding school application assistance. Students also have access to individual subject tutorials, as well as organizational- and study-skills support. Students who have nearly completed their high school requirements can earn college credit for classes hosted on the ASR campus taught by contractual arrangement with one of the local colleges.

We are proud of our academic program at ASR: Traditional curricula geared toward university study, combined with integrative approaches to learning and therapeutic work, as well as individualized supplemental services, allow us to stand out as the leader among college preparatory therapeutic boarding schools.

The three phases of our school’s therapeutic curriculum are designed as follows:

I. Orientation/Assessment Phase – “Pathways”

This is the initial phase of our program and lasts approximately 3 weeks, although individual student needs may require a longer time frame and shorter durations are also feasible.

The focus and purpose of the Pathways phase is to orient the student to the Academy, assess the student’s social, academic, and therapeutic needs and skills, and begin to have the student understand the areas of his/her life that need to be explored and worked on

11

while they are at the Academy. Outdoor-based, experiential activities are traditionally used and the themes of this part of our program are acceptance, trust and honesty.

Academic services during this phase include a learning styles needs assessment, academic coaching, and preparation related to full integration into a regular class schedule. Finally, a primary counselor is assigned to monitor the student’s overall psychological, developmental, and interpersonal growth and to facilitate group, individual and family therapy as well as communication to parents and referral sources. As the student ‘commences’ into the main campus program, a 2-hour meeting is scheduled with the parents and the primary counselor to outline expectations and program components going forward.

II. Lower and Upper School Phases

Upon graduation from Pathways, students are now fully engaged into their academic schedules and are part of the “main campus.” Students work through their academic course work through individualized and traditional means, substance abuse/dependency services continue as do individual, family and group counseling. Major elements and therapeutic foci of this piece of the 15-month program include –

• Adherence to group and community norms and rules – many of which are designed and reinforced by students and student leaders themselves.

• “Life Steps” – Five separate personal development seminars that cover topics such as Anger, Personal Accountability and Patterns, Self-esteem and Positive Image of Self, and Re-integration into the family system. Our current program includes five Life Steps, which are each followed by a Family Resolution that builds on the topics and skills reviewed in the Life Step.

• A “Personal Challenge” (occurs before the 4th Family Resolution) – a presentation on a topic of personal reflection and of a personal challenge to the student. Topics include singing a song, reciting a poem to an audience, acting out a role-play with parents, etc.

• “Family Resolutions” – these are intermittent multi-family group sessions conducted during our program. Students who have completed a Life Step are then prepared for the corresponding Family Resolution where their parents and siblings come to campus for the Resolution. With each Family Resolution, time with family/parents is increased from on-site visits to multi-day visits off campus/at home.

• Medication Management – we have a contractual relationship with child and adolescent psychiatrists with many years of experience treating students at ASR.

• Substance Abuse Services – we have an full-time, Masters level substance abuse counselor and a part-time counselor who conduct individual, group and special substance abuse services such as Relapse Prevention, 12-step groups, administers the SASSI. In addition to this, all of our counselors work with our students on substance abuse issues based on their individual needs.

• Experiential and Adventure Based Programming – part of ASR’s history and traditions includes a robust student life program. We are proud to have an on-campus

12

ropes course, an indoor climbing cave, and many outdoor oriented recreational activities where we take advantage of all this area of Western New England has to offer.

• Clubs – special clubs are a key ingredient to a student’s social and physical as well as academic enhancement. We offer several clubs that change with the seasons including landscape design, snow boarding, golf, philosophy, theater, and a capella groups.

Program Phase Themes:

After completing the Pathways Orientation phase, students move into the main campus program and work to prepare for the first Life Step. Students are considered in “Lower School I” during this portion of the program. Students work on accountability, responsibility, fortitude within and between their teams and peers during Lower School I. This part of the program lasts approximately 6 weeks and culminates with the first Family Resolution. The theme of the Family Resolution builds on that of the Life Step, which is character. Students and parents remain on campus for a 3 day program guided by ASR staff.

After completing the first Family Resolution, students are moved into “Lower School II.” The themes worked on during this phase are connections, belonging, and interdependence. As with the previous phase, Lower School II culminates with a Life Step embodying these themes and then a Family Resolution that focuses on family. After spending a day on campus, students are allowed for the first time to go off campus and spend 3 nights in a hotel with their families.

Upon returning to campus from the hotel visit, students will then be placed into “Upper School I.” This phase of our program focuses on integrity and identity. A third Life Step and subsequent Family Resolution culminate this phase and focuses on “expectations.” After completing a day on campus, parents who participate in the 3rd Family Resolution may take their student off campus to the first home visit. This usually is a 4-5 day visit to practice skills and communication strategies within the family.

When students return from their home visit they are now ready for the final phase of our program prior to transitions and graduation. This fourth phase, “Upper School II” focuses on generosity and forgiveness. Students complete their academic work and work towards the 4th Life Step and Family Resolution where the theme of forgiveness presides. Students then leave campus for a 7-10 day home visit where preparing for returning post graduation will be worked on. Some students will attend local AA/NA or other support groups while on this home visit. Finalizing college applications and visits may also occur.

Finally, students are ready for practicing skills that will enable them to integrate successfully back into the family and prepare them for post-ASR school whether that be college or another secondary school placement. Since each student and family’s needs are unique, the counseling and academic staff at ASR will tailor the final work for

13

individual students. For example, a student may need more time to practice family communication skills or may need some additional academic credit for community service. These can all be addressed and arranged in order to help complete the individual plan originally developed and revised along the way for each student.

III. Graduation & Alumni Services

A diploma from ASR currently requires twenty-six credits, including four credit years of English and three credits years of Math, Laboratory Science, and Social Science. Formal graduation ceremonies are currently scheduled throughout the year and are powerfully emotional events.

Once students successfully complete our 15-month program, they receive a year’s worth of Alumni Services including receiving a regular newsletter, after care planning, periodic consultation, and special events many times co-organized by alumni and alumni parents and family. A full-time Alumni Counselor provides a link between the academy and alumni. This piece of our program is perhaps one of the most unique within the industry and something that solidifies the work students and families have done over the course of stay and helps ensure life-long success.

Prior Outdoor Therapeutic Experience Strongly Recommended

We strongly recommend that all students who wish to attend the Academy at Swift River successfully complete an outdoor therapeutic program experience (i.e. “wilderness” program) prior to enrollment. The program does not need to be an Aspen Education Group program.

Current data on where students are coming to ASR from shows that almost 60% have completed an outdoor therapeutic intervention immediately prior to enrollment at ASR. This, in of itself, identifies the student population we are organizing ourselves to serve with our clinically-based, therapeutic 15 month program. However, not all students have had that benefit of the introspection and personal development offered in a therapeutic wilderness program. Additionally, we have determined that the majority of the students who come to ASR without first completing a wilderness program are not in the appropriate mind-set to build on and affirm the accountability that students who complete a wilderness program have. The fact that a good outdoor program provides the opportunity for adolescents to accept some accountability for their actions is key for them to be successful during their stay at ASR. Students, parents, and staff alike all agree to the emotional benefits of a wilderness experience before ASR.

Given that the majority of our population at ASR comes from the North East, ASR has been working very closely with our sister program, Adirondack Leadership Expeditions

(ALE) in Saranac Lake, NY to develop a smooth transition and define and develop a program whereby students who are enrolled at ALE and are appropriate for a therapeutic school placement, can come to ASR. ALE will prepare students bound for ASR with those skills to ensure they are ready for academics and a co-ed, socially complex campus life. This collaboration will result in the development of specific goals to be accomplished at the wilderness program that will enable students to be successful at ASR. This is exciting as we are the first of the Aspen Education Group programs to embark on defining a relationship in this fashion.

The Team Model

The community of ASR is divided into five Teams- the Eagles, Cougars, Wolves, Mustangs, and Buffalo. Immediately upon arriving at ASR, each student is assigned to a team (usually according to which clinician we feel the student might work best with) with whom they will stay for the duration of their time at ASR. Each team includes anywhere from 15-25 students and serves as a support group for the students. Students in each team are at all different points in the program (i.e. some may be brand new to ASR and others may be close to graduation), therefore providing a great peer-mentorship model.

Teams participate in group therapy together one to two times a week. They also participate in team activities and leadership events together. The team model provides us with an opportunity to teach team building and student to student mentoring. Each team has also developed a value system and special traditions that they share. For example, the Buffalo team has a campsite in the woods that they built, and when a new student joins their team they take them out there to sign their team sign. This is a nice welcoming gesture for all new Buffalo.

There are many staff assigned to each team which allows us to have a very holistic approach to working with our students. Each team has at least one clinician, one residential life supervisor, two teachers, and two residential life mentors assigned to it. Through regular weekly meetings, we are able to make sure that each student is getting his academic, clinical, and residential needs met. Families also have a network of people who know their children well and can help with all types of support.

Benefits of the Team Model approach:

• Enhanced interdepartmental communication

• Better follow through and consistency (students will be less able to divide staff)

• Improved relationships as a smaller number of staff will come to more deeply know a manageable number of students

• Increased opportunities for positive peer role modeling as new students will be integrated with upper class students

• Improved integration between residential, therapeutic and academic services (a best practice standard).

Principles for Working Together at ASR

The following principles guide the staff at ASR as we work together to best support your child. They are valuable principles for all relationships in life, and we encourage you to integrate them into your own life.

Truth and authenticity with each other. This is the most important and essential principle for all groups and relationships. Without this we will accomplish very little.

Mindfulness. We should encourage and facilitate self-awareness.

Non-pejorative behaviors. We are equals in the world of mind, soul, and heart. We should assume that everyone is doing the best they can and we should help each other to do this. Avoid language and labels that are critical.

Inclusion. We should monitor non-verbal behaviors and try to include everyone in the group every time we meet. It is more important for everyone to feel present and included than to have just a few participants talking to one another.

One thing at a time. We should try to discuss one issue thoroughly before going on to another topic. This will provide a sense of order and completion to our work without requiring a specific outcome for every issue.

Avoid quick solutions. We should explore all sides of an issue knowing that the solution may be contained in a compromise or a combination of several viewpoints.

Holistic world view. Things contain their opposite. We are responsible for calling out the dialect, seeking the wholeness, and discovering the middle truth that includes both points of view.

Additional principles. We should be open to the need for additional principles as we progress.

Therapeutic Curriculum

The therapeutic curriculum at ASR has helped us achieve national recognition as one of the top boarding schools. Our commitment to honesty, integrity, and self-discovery has changed both students, parents, and staff in profound ways. As you progress through our program with your child, you will have the opportunity to experience groups and Lifesteps through the eyes of your child. You will actively participate in Family Resolutions and will be invited to your child’s Personal Challenge. We encourage you to delve into your own personal self-exploration as your child shares his growth with you.

All students receive:

• Individualized goal-attainment approach based on needs assessment

• Weekly individual counseling session

• Weekly treatment groups

• Substance abuse evaluation, motivational-enhancement treatments, mutual help groups, and relapse prevention

• Specialized treatment groups including conflict and anger management groups, expressive group therapy, adoption and grief groups

• Five intensive theme-focused seminars (Lifesteps) to promote psychological and relational development

• Series of multi-family groups (Resolutions) that address topics such as responsibility, expectations, parenting style, forgiveness, and reintegration

• Medical consultation and management

Lifesteps

Lifesteps are full day emotional workshops that are the heart and soul of our curriculum. There are five Lifesteps- one for each quarter of the program. The first focuses on anger and fear, the second on insecurities and cover-ups, the third on shame and finding your true self, and the fourth on forgiveness and the fifth on \*\*\*\*. Parents will experience portions of the Lifesteps when they visit ASR for Family Resolutions.

Family Resolutions

Family Resolutions are when the parents and/or siblings come to visit their children, meet the staff, and participate in family group therapy. This happens five times during a child’s program. All the parents assemble in the Great Room at 9:00 without students present for talks with mentors, clinicians, and other staff. After lunch, their children join them and the group is broken into groups of approximately 4 families to engage in group

20

therapy. During this, families talk about issues at home, work through issues that were taught in the last Lifestep, and create plans for when the students graduate. Families often help each other in profound ways through their shared experiences. After each Resolution, there is a visit that follows. The first visit is on-campus. Parents stay in local hotels and visit their children each day for 3 days. The second visit is at a local hotel. It is the first time the kids go off campus for a visit for 3 days. The third, fourth, and fifth visits are home visits. We arrange the visits this way so that students are slowly reintegrated into their lives at home and can practice using the tools they are learning. Often kids get very scared and regress during these Resolutions and visits, and it is our job to help them work through their fears and set positive coping skills.

Sometimes under special occasions, we have mini-resolutions. This occurs when there is an urgent family issue that must be dealt with immediately and cannot wait until the scheduled resolution. It can also be used as an intervention if a child is in jeopardy of being asked to leave ASR.

Personal Challenge

Personal Challenges occur after the fourth Lifestep. Resembling a talent show, each student has a specific task that they are assigned to perform in front of the whole school and their parents. Staff chooses what the students do and it is designed to help them show the world an aspect of their true self that they usually struggle to live with. For instance, a very shy student might have to dance and sing an upbeat song. The class clown might need to dress in a suit and recite a Martin Luther King speech. The Personal Challenge night is designed to give the kids an opportunity to shine and do something they never thought that they could do. The whole community embraces them and celebrates their strength and courage. Group Therapy

Groups are held three times a week. During groups, kids meet in groups of approximately 15 kids with 2 staff and sit in a circle. Students process personal issues, family successes and difficulties, and social issues at ASR. We give very direct feedback to students and help them to share their feelings around issues that caused them to be at ASR. Groups are emotional and sacred spaces where we ask they students to share very intimate details of their lives.. One day a week groups are run by teams. These groups are emotional and focus on family issues, Lifesteps, and Resolutions. Occasionally we have Mixed Group, which is a time for kids to request to talk to other kids with whom they have issues. These groups are mostly behavioral and about conflict resolution. Reliable and compassionate Alumni Services Counselors support ASR families to realize their fundamental values, to maintain a high degree of personal safety, to develop healthy and responsible relationships, and to keep hope alive. • Mutual Respect

Native American Sweat Lodges at ASR

Our students are provided several opportunities to participate in Sweat Lodges while at ASR. Sweat Lodges are beautifully spiritual rituals that solidify teams and often help students to get in touch with a very deep part of themselves. They are physically and emotionally challenging and are a great honor to participate in. It is very important that we treat this Native American ritual with utmost respect and dignity. Our Sweat Lodges are run by ASR staff who are members of the Lakota and Blackfoot tribes.

“Where did it come from?”

Indigenous people throughout the world have used purification through ritualized cleansing for centuries. Examples are found on all inhabited continents, including the origins of the Finnish sauna and the mikvah of Judaism. Saunas and sweat lodges can be found in many parts of the world: most of North and Central America, Ireland, much of Europe, Russia, Africa, Japan, the Mediterranean and the Middle East.

In this country, the original 500 Nations of native peoples each have (or had) distinct languages, customs and livelihoods. It should come as no surprise that vast differences exist in spiritual practices as well, and so it is with “Sweat Lodge”. Although most tribes practice purification rituals, and most of those include some form of sweat bath, it is important to note that there is not one way, practice, or authority when it comes to Ceremony.

Tribal elders and oral histories tell us that the sweat lodge has been part of our lives for ten thousand years. This is a very old, sacred ceremony, with a powerful, but at times difficult past. Until the passage of the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978, sweat lodges and other native spiritual practices were outlawed, and countless people have been persecuted and died for their beliefs and practices. Since 1978 (and, underground, earlier), Native American sweat lodges have spread throughout the world as more cultures have been given exposure to this form of meditative, ritualized cleansing. I point this out so that it is understood that as we approach the Sweat Lodge, we do so in a reverent manner – this is not a hobby, nor a historical reenactment, nor an anachronism.

“What is it?”

The Lakota word for Sweat Lodge is inipi (“in-NEE-pee”), which is a contraction of the words inyan “stone” and tipi “lodge”: The Grandfather Stone Lodge.

A typical Sweat Lodge has three basic physical components: the fire pit, where rocks are heated, the lodge itself, which looks like a rounded tent-like structure, covered in blankets and tarps, and the “altar”, a mound of dirt between the Lodge and the Fire.

Three figures play key roles in a Sweat: the fireman – or fire keeper – who tends the fire, carries rocks, and is watchful for the people attending, the water-pourer – or

27

Sweat Leader – who is someone who has been trained in the ways of ceremony and has been given the right to conduct sweats, and the People – those who have come to purify, cleanse, pray, etc.

“What goes on inside a Lodge?”

People sit inside the lodge, men on one side, women on the other, and the heated rocks are brought in. The door to the lodge is closed, so that it is dark and hot inside. Water is sprinkled on the rocks, creating steam. People talk, meditate, pray, chant, sing as needed. This happens four times, for four “rounds” total, which usually takes around two hours total.

“How hot does it get?”

Hot enough to make you want to leave, and hot enough to make you want to stay. Yes, it gets very hot – Sweat Lodge is not meant to be a comfortable, cushy experience. It requires effort, endurance, humility, giving and receiving support -- all of which can cause discomfort. However, it is not life-threatening nor medically compromising (indeed, it is healing).

“You say people pray in Sweat Lodge. Is it against my child’s religion to attend Sweat Lodge?”

Attending Sweat Lodge should pose no spiritual, religious, or ethical challenges to any person’s faith. I have sweat with ministers, priests, and rabbis, as well as agnostics, atheists, Buddhists, B’Hai and practitioners of many spiritual practices. The sweat is about purification, in whatever way is meaningful to the person attending. The sweat is also about supporting each other and coming together for a common cause. Surely this should pose no threat to any religious (or non-religious) ideology.

“Is attending Sweat Lodge a requirement of the ASR Program?

Absolutely not. No student is every forced or coerced into going into the Sweat Lodge, and sometimes a student chooses not to. However, the vast majority of students appreciate and enjoy the Lodge, and work to hold it with the respect and dignity it deserves.

“How does the Sweat Lodge help my child’s therapeutic treatment?”

Students benefit in many ways from the Sweat Lodge. Simply attending and enduring four rounds in the lodge gives many people a renewed confidence they have not known. It helps bring focus to the connection between our bodies, thoughts, emotions, and how each of these contribute to our sense of integrated self. The Sweat Lodge provides a context whereby individuals are brought face to face with their actions, the results of their actions, and their responsibilities to themselves and others. Students also experience how to request, receive, and give support to each other in healthy ways which contributes to each person’s growth.

Parallels have been found between effective group therapy and Native American Sweat Lodge (Garrett and Osborne, 1995). There is a growing body of research literature which examines the relationship between therapeutic group process and its augmentation

28

through ritual and ceremony. We at ASR believe that the Sweat Lodge provides a unique, effective experience which supports students’ clinical treatment by offering opportunities for insight matched by congruent, positive behaviors.

Questions about the Sweat Lodge at ASR can be directed to Josh Becker, Director of Academics, jbecker@swiftriver.com, extension 249.

Student Mail

Letters between families are an essential part of our emotional growth work at ASR and should be used as a tool to improve family connections. Both parents and the student are expected to write regular letters that include both small talk and deeper emotional content. Sometimes families will receive “homework” from the counselors working with their child and will be asked to write letters addressing specific issues within the family.

Each student has a mail slot located in the phone room. Mentors are responsible for distributing mail on a daily basis and students can claim their mail during phone room hours. All mail that students send out and receive must be read and approved by their counselor. Counselors look for appropriate content and ensure that the person the student is writing to/ receiving a letter from is approved. Any mail to be sent out must be turned in to the counselor in an unsealed and addressed envelope. We do this in order to help families process difficulties between one another and to push students to be as honest as possible in their communication.

All of the ASR agreements must be observed in letter writing- they may not war-story about drug/alcohol use, sex, or violence. Students may not swear in their letters. If they do, we explain to them why this is not appropriate and have them rewrite the letter. If the communication style in the letter is unhealthy (such as threatening, blaming, etc), we use this as an opportunity to counsel the student or family member and have them rewrite the letter with more constructive communication. Students and families should be addressing the particular topics of the part of the program they are at- trust/anger/fear, insecurities and cover-ups, shames and games, and forgiveness. You will be coached about how to do so by the counselor working with you.

As students progress through the program, they earn the opportunity to write to more people. The process is as follows:

• When first arriving on campus, students may only write to parents, siblings, and grandparents.

• After the first Resolution, they may write to extended family- aunts, uncles, etc.

• After the second Resolution, students may write a proposal to write to 3 friends from home. These friends must be approved by their parents and counselors. The purpose of writing to these friends is to reestablish and improve support systems for when the student returns home. These 3 friends are the only friends that the student will be allowed to see on their first home visit and this is contingent on healthy communication in their letters. Students are expected to share their emotional growth work with their friends, create new healthy boundaries, and ask for support in following ASRs agreements and the student’s personal values.

• After the first home visit, students may write to 2 more approved friends. They will be able to see all 5 friends on their next home visit. Again, this is contingent on the fact that emotional, healthy letters have been both sent

Restrictions

During your child’s stay at ASR, it is likely that he or she will be put on a restriction at some point. Restrictions are sometimes reactions to behavior breaches, and sometimes are solely to help a student stay on track with emotional work. In cases of behavior breaches, restrictions provide students with one-on-one time with staff to process the situation and to look at the emotional chain of events that causes them to react poorly in some situations. Through this processing students learn how to recognize their triggers and how to better cope with their feelings. Many students go on multiple restrictions while they are at ASR.

Description of Restrictions:

Restrictions are generally given for breaking one of the major agreements: sex, drugs, or violence. Restrictions are also given for major dishonesty or safety breeches.

Restrictions are writing assignments that are kept in a folder. There are 3 different levels depending on the severity of the behavior that warranted it. The types of writing assignments are the same for any level- it is only the length and other consequences that change.

• Reflections. Reflections are the least serious of the restrictions. They are kept in a blue folder and are generally 3-7 days. Reflections can be used non-punitively if a student needs extra therapeutic support. Some students will request to be on reflections. Reflections can also be used for minor behavioral problems and can be combined with work projects if necessary. Reflections generally do not have other consequences attached to them.

• Challenges. Challenges are the most common restriction. They are kept in a red folder and are generally 7-10 days long. Challenges are appropriate for most behavioral problems. Students on challenges will provided with writing assignments to complete by a certain date. They will complete one full weekend of work projects.

• Self Studies. Self Studies are the most severe consequence that we have at ASR. They are kept in black folders and are usually 10-12 days. Students should only be placed on a SS if they have repeatedly broken agreements, have broken more than one of the major agreements, ran away, etc. Self Studies lose all free time, have work projects and early wake-up every weekend, and have many bans.

Writing Assignments:

51

Restrictions are usually designed around the “Think, Feel, Do” model. The goal of a restriction is to help the student understand how their behaviors are influenced by how they feel, and to learn to manage their feelings in healthier ways.

Beginning: THINK. The first group of assignments help the student examine what they were thinking during the period of time when the behavior occurred. This usually coincides with the part of the program they are at. Newer students might be thinking they can’t trust anyone, or that everyone hates them. Older students might be able to talk about their insecurities and patterns more openly. We help them process what stressful things are going on in their lives at this time, and what they think about it.

Middle: FEEL. The large middle section of the restriction focuses on how the student was really feeling during the time of that negative behavior. It is our job to help the student understand that their feelings of anger, sadness, loneliness, etc. are really deeper than just the situation that caused the behavior. Built up anger often becomes explosive, build up sadness can cause depression. This is our chance to help the student purge this pain. Writing assignments push them to reflect on other times in their life when they felt this way. This is when we might incorporate their history of adoption, loss, eating disorders, etc. The students are often in an emotional state for several days and quite often regress back into negative behavior. When they are emotionally overwhelmed, they often don’t know healthy coping skills and may begin to break agreements again. This is expected and is a great opportunity for growth through conversation. It is important to exercise compassion and patience during this period. This is a good sign that your assignments are making emotional connections within the student. This is when it is very important that the student is having serious appointments with students and staff to talk about these assignments. They are also encouraged to share their work with their families when they feel ready.

End: DO. Before closing the restriction, it is important to gently pull a student out of his intense feelings. We do this by transitioning our assignments back to the behavior. We have the student write about how their feelings drove them to act out, and how they have done this in the past. How do those behaviors affect their thoughts about themselves, their families, and their friends? How do those behaviors (the DO) drive them back into the negative THINKING and FEELING? We show them the cycle- this is when the light bulb should be going off over their heads. Once they understand this, we teach them some healthy ways to cope with their feelings. We have them practice asking for help, talking in groups, and sharing with their families. The restriction ends with them writing a commitment to use their new coping skills and the goals that will help them do so.

Student Belongings Policies

Taking care of student belongings at ASR is a monumental and important task. We ask all families to help us with this by limiting the number of items each child has at ASR.
