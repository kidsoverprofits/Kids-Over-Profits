How to research a facility for a profile, section by section. Converted from
the Google Doc "Guide to Creating Facility Profiles" (owner's Drive, last
edited 2026-09-17). The doc was the source until now; edit this copy from here on.

## General Research Tips

### Use the SCIAD Zotero Library for Source Material

An extensive curated database of articles, reports, and academic references
related to the industry:
[SCIAD Zotero Library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/4552235/sciad_net/library)

- Use the search bar to look up facility names, staff, parent companies, or
  therapy types.
- Browse tags or folders for topic-specific research.
- Many entries include source links, publication details, and related context.

### How to Do a Corporate Lookup

1. Google: `"[state name]" + "business entity search"` or
   `"[state name]" + "corporate record search"`
2. Click the official state government website (usually ends in .gov).
3. Search the facility name (try variations, former names, or owner names).
4. Download or view available filings: Articles of Incorporation, amendments,
   DBAs, owner names, and status (active/dissolved).
5. If nothing comes up, try [OpenCorporates](https://opencorporates.com) or
   search using known owners or umbrella orgs.

### How to Search by Filetype and Source

1. Use Google advanced search operators to find specific kinds of documents:
   - `filetype:pdf`: only PDFs
   - `filetype:doc` or `filetype:docx`: Word documents
   - `filetype:xls` or `filetype:xlsx`: spreadsheets
2. To limit by domain (e.g., government or education):
   - `site:.gov`, `site:.edu`, or `site:.org`
   - Example: `"Spring Ridge Academy" filetype:pdf site:.org`
3. To find older documents or sources from specific platforms, add
   `site:archive.org`, `site:issuu.com`, or `site:strugglingteens.com`.

## Confirming Facility Name and Tracking Name Changes

**Starting point:** You already have the name of the facility you're
researching. This step is about confirming it and identifying any name
changes or DBAs over time.

### 1. Track Yearly Name Changes in NATSAP Directories

- If the facility is a **NATSAP member**, pull the **annual NATSAP member
  directories**.
- **Compare listings year by year** to see if the facility's name, listed
  owner, or contact info changes.
- Note any new names, merged entries, or rebrands.

### 2. Find Corporate Records

- Google: `"[state name]" + "corporate record search"` (e.g., Utah corporate
  record search).
- Follow the official state link to access business entity or corporate
  filings.
- If records are **free**, download available PDFs for registration history,
  DBAs, or amendments.
- If records are **not free**, check to see if another watchdog group or open
  records site already has them.

### 3. Check Archived Websites with the Wayback Machine

- For facilities **not in NATSAP**, go to
  [archive.org/web](https://archive.org/web).
- Search the facility's website URL and browse **snapshots one year at a
  time**.
- Look at **headers, About pages, footers, and contact sections** for any
  mention of:
  - Rebranding
  - Ownership shifts
  - "Formerly known as" references

### Confirming Facility Address

**Starting point:** Most addresses are already known. Only use this process if
an address is missing, unclear, or needs verification.

#### 1. Search on [npidb.org](https://npidb.org)

- Enter the facility name or known staff names (e.g., directors or doctors).
- Look for facility addresses tied to National Provider Identifiers (NPIs).

#### 2. Check Licensing Records

- Search the facility on your **state's licensing or health department
  portal**.
- Look for **inspection reports**, **license applications**, or **compliance
  documents** that include full addresses.

#### 3. Use Google Maps

- Search the facility name or any known physical landmarks.
- Use satellite and street view to confirm signage or match old photos.
- Combine with names from court documents or survivor reports to spot
  historical addresses.

### Establishing the Operational Timeline

**Goal:** Confirm founding year, closure year (or whether the program is still
operating), and any major changes like rebrands or mergers.

#### 1. Check *Woodbury Reports* ([strugglingteens.com](http://strugglingteens.com))

- Search the archives for announcements of **new program launches**,
  **closings**, **mergers**, or **leadership changes**.
- Especially useful for facilities from the 1990s to 2010s.

#### 2. Compare NATSAP Member Directories

- If the facility is a NATSAP member, review **annual directories** year by
  year.
- Look for the **first appearance** (founding indicator) and **last
  appearance** (possible closure/rebrand).

#### 3. Use the Wayback Machine

- Search the facility's domain at [archive.org/web](https://archive.org/web).
- Track **when the site first became active** and **when it stopped updating
  or disappeared**.
- Check for transitional pages that mention being "closed," "rebranded," or
  "merged."

#### 4. Review Corporate Filings

- Use the state's corporate record database to check **formation and
  dissolution dates**.
- Look for **amendments** or **name changes** filed over time.

#### 5. Search for News Coverage

- Look for local news articles reporting on **program openings**,
  **closures**, or **scandals**.
- Use terms like "[program name] closes," "[program name] founded," or
  "[program name] opens doors."

## Program Overview

**Type**: [e.g. RTC, Wilderness, Therapeutic Boarding School, Group Home]

### Facility Type Spotting Guide

Use this guide to identify the type of facility you're researching based on
language, structure, and context clues. These categories are not mutually
exclusive, but most programs fit primarily into one.

**1. Residential Treatment Centers / Facilities (RTC/RTF)**

- **Look for:** Terms like "residential treatment," "clinical team," "QPTRF,"
  "licensed mental health professionals"
- **Common indicators:** Medication management, individual/family therapy,
  treatment plans
- **Licensing:** Often licensed through state departments of health or mental
  health

**2. Therapeutic Group Homes**

- **Look for:** "Group home," "family-style living," "small staff-to-resident
  ratio"
- **Common indicators:** Located in residential neighborhoods, typically fewer
  than 12 youth
- **Licensing:** State-level residential child care licenses

**3. Juvenile Detention Centers**

- **Look for:** "Youth detention," "court-ordered placement," "juvenile
  justice"
- **Common indicators:** Locked doors, correctional staff, direct court
  involvement
- **Licensing:** State/county juvenile justice or corrections

**4. Wilderness Programs**

- **Look for:** "Expedition," "field guides," "primitive skills," "base camp"
- **Common indicators:** Outdoor sleeping arrangements, hiking long distances,
  remote locations
- **Licensing:** Sometimes licensed as treatment; sometimes education or camp
  loopholes

**5. Therapeutic / "Emotional Growth" Boarding Schools**

- **Look for:** "Therapeutic boarding school," "emotional growth," "holistic
  education"
- **Common indicators:** Academic program plus therapy, long-term stays,
  behavior-based progress
- **Licensing:** Often dual licensed or only as private schools

**6. ASD and I/DD-Specific Programs**

- **Look for:** "Autism program," "developmental disabilities," "ABA therapy,"
  "life skills"
- **Common indicators:** Focus on daily living and communication; may use
  restraint-heavy behavior plans
- **Licensing:** Varies; can be ICF/IID licensed or group home structure

**7. Religious Homes or Academies**

- **Look for:** "Christian boarding school," "faith-based recovery," "reform
  home," "biblical discipline"
- **Common indicators:** Bible study, daily chapel, obedience framed as
  spiritual growth
- **Licensing:** Often unlicensed or operate under religious exemptions

**8. Adolescent Substance Use Disorder (SUD) Rehab Facilities**

- **Look for:** "Teen rehab," "12-step program," "sober living," "dual
  diagnosis"
- **Common indicators:** Focus on drug/alcohol recovery, use of AA/NA
  language, relapse prevention
- **Licensing:** Licensed as addiction treatment or mental health facilities

**9. Youth Ranches**

- **Look for:** "Therapeutic ranch," "working ranch," "equine therapy"
- **Common indicators:** Physical labor, chores as therapy, remote/rural
  setting, may overlap with wilderness or religious themes
- **Licensing:** Often minimal; may claim religious or agricultural exemptions

**10. Psychiatric Hospitals**

- **Look for:** "Acute inpatient," "psychiatric stabilization," "locked ward"
- **Common indicators:** Short-term crisis care, suicide watch, on-site
  medical staff, involuntary commitment possible
- **Licensing:** Hospital-level accreditation (e.g., Joint Commission),
  licensed psychiatric facility

**11. Boot Camps / Military-Style Academies**

- **Look for:** "Drill instructor," "cadet," "military-style discipline,"
  "boot camp for teens"
- **Common indicators:** Uniforms, marching, physical drills, verbal abuse
  framed as toughness
- **Licensing:** Often unlicensed or minimal oversight; some contract with
  state justice systems

When in doubt, compare language across sources (website, survivor accounts,
licenses, staff bios) to triangulate the true model. Many programs disguise or
rebrand their model to appear more therapeutic or academic than it really is.

1. **Fundamentalist Christianity**
   *Examples:* Roloff Homes, Teen Challenge

**Known Referrers**: [e.g. IECA consultants, courts, clergy, schools]

### First Step: Define What Counts as a Referrer

A *referrer* is anyone or any entity that sends kids to the program. This
includes:

- **Independent Educational Consultants (IECs)**
- **School districts** (via IEP placements or out-of-district referrals)
- **Juvenile justice** (judges, probation officers, diversion programs)
- **Therapists or social workers**
- **Clergy or religious leaders**
- **Parents' employers** or **insurance companies** (for EAP or covered
  treatment)

### Then Start with These Research Angles

#### 1. Archived Website Content

- Use the Wayback Machine to review FAQs, admissions pages, and "About Us"
  sections.
- Look for phrases like:
  - *"Recommended by top educational consultants"*
  - *"We accept students from across the country through court or school
    referrals."*

#### 2. Consultant Directories

- Search the [IECA](https://www.iecaonline.com/) directory and archived NATSAP
  member consultant lists.
- Look for programs mentioned in consultant bios or "program visit" blogs.
- Try phrases in Google like:
  - `"visited [program name]" site:iecaonline.com`
  - `"recommended [program name]" consultant`

#### 3. Lawsuits and Depositions

- Legal filings sometimes name who referred a student.
- Deposition transcripts may include details about how a student ended up at
  the facility.

#### 4. Survivor Accounts

- Look for blogs, Reddit posts, or interviews where survivors say *how* they
  got there.
- Watch for mentions of consultants, pastors, school officials, or
  therapists.

#### 5. Local School District or Court Contracts

- Use public records requests to check if the facility had contracts with
  public agencies.
- FOIA requests or state open records portals can turn up placement info.

## Legal and Regulatory History

**Lawsuits**: [Linked case summaries]

### Lawsuit Lookup Guide

Use this guide to locate lawsuits involving a facility, its staff, or its
parent company. These steps apply if the case isn't already documented in
Zotero or linked in previous research.

#### Step 1: Start with Google

- Search combinations like:
  - `"[facility name]" + lawsuit OR sued OR complaint OR court`
  - `"[staff name]" + lawsuit OR deposition OR court case`
  - `"[company name]" + class action OR abuse lawsuit`
- Look for press coverage, legal blogs, docket references, or named
  plaintiffs/defendants.

#### Step 2: Check State Court Portals

- Google: `[state name] + case search` or `[state name] + court records`
- Look for:
  - Civil or criminal case search
  - Juvenile/family court records (sometimes restricted)
- Search by:
  - Facility name
  - Staff or owner names
  - Known attorneys or law firms

#### Step 3: Use PACER for Federal Cases

- Site: <https://pacer.uscourts.gov>
- Create a free account (only pay for documents viewed/downloaded).
- Use the **Case Locator** to search by:
  - Facility or staff name
  - Parent company
- Look for civil rights violations, ADA claims, or class actions.

#### Step 4: Try Judyrecords

- Site: <https://judyrecords.com>
- Free access to state and local court records.
- Search by:
  - Staff name
  - Facility name
  - Location
- Filter by state, court type, or date range.

#### Step 5: Check Other Legal Aggregators

1. **CourtListener (RECAP)**
   - <https://www.courtlistener.com>
   - Community-uploaded PACER dockets and filings
2. **PlainSite**
   - <https://www.plainsite.org>
   - Tracks litigation history by company or individual
3. **Dockets Justia**
   - <https://dockets.justia.com>
   - Summarizes federal lawsuits by topic and party
4. **County Court Websites**
   - Google the county + "court records" or "clerk of court"
   - Some allow name-based searching with free docket access
5. **Law360 (optional)**
   - Use headline summaries to trace active or high-profile cases
   - Google example: `"[facility name]" site:law360.com`

**Inspections / Citations**: [e.g. DHHS, fire marshal, licensing agencies]

### How to Check for Inspections and Citations

#### Step 1: Identify the State Licensing Agency

- Most residential programs are regulated by the **Department of Health and
  Human Services (DHHS)** or **Department of Children and Families (DCF)**.
- Google:
  - `"licensing lookup site:.gov [state name]"`
  - or `"child care licensing [state name]"`

#### Step 2: Use the State's License Lookup Tool

- Many states provide searchable databases for:
  - Residential treatment centers
  - Group homes
  - Child care or behavioral health providers
- Search by facility name, city, or license number (if known).
- Look for inspection reports, complaints, or license status (active,
  suspended, expired).

#### Step 3: Review the Reports

- Reports may include:
  - **Deficiencies or violations**
  - **Corrective actions required**
  - **Dates of inspection**
  - **Incident investigations**
- Some states rate severity or risk level of violations.

#### Step 4: Supplement with News Coverage

- Search:
  - `"[facility name]" + DHHS OR DCF OR citation OR inspection site:.gov`
  - `"[facility name]" + inspection report OR child abuse OR violation`

#### Step 5: File a Public Records Request (if needed)

- If the database is incomplete or missing documents, file a FOIA request with
  the state agency.
- Ask for:
  - "All inspection reports and complaint investigations for [facility name]
    from [date range]."

## How to Use Libraries and Archives

Libraries hold what Google can't find: undigitized local newspapers, clipping
files, brochures, and records from programs that closed before the internet.
Start here when a facility operated before about 2000, or when online searches
dry up.

### 1. Start with a Librarian

- Call, email, or use "Ask a Librarian" chat at the public library in the town
  or county where the facility operated. Many reference librarians will do
  short lookups for remote researchers for free.
- Be specific. Give the facility name, any former names, the town, and the
  years you're researching. Say what you're looking for (news coverage, ads,
  zoning disputes, obituaries of staff).
- Ask what they have that isn't in the online catalog: clipping files, local
  history rooms, indexes to the local paper, or city directories.
- Ask who else to contact: the county historical society, the state library,
  or a nearby university archive.
- You don't have to explain why. "I'm researching the history of a local youth
  program" is enough.

Sample email:

> I'm researching [facility name], a youth residential program that operated
> in [town] from about [years]. Does your library have clipping files, local
> newspaper indexes, or other materials on it? I'm not local, so I'd
> appreciate any lookups or copies you can provide, and I'm happy to pay copy
> fees.

### 2. Check Vertical Files (Clipping Files)

Vertical files are folders of newspaper clippings, pamphlets, flyers, and
ephemera, sorted by subject. They often hold things that exist nowhere else,
like a program's promotional brochure or a church bulletin announcing its
opening.

- Ask the local public library and the county historical society if they keep
  vertical files.
- Ask for folders under several headings: the facility name, former names, the
  founder's name, "schools, private," "youth programs," "juvenile
  delinquency," or the road or area where it was located.
- Vertical files are rarely cataloged online, so you usually have to ask a
  librarian or visit in person.
- Photograph every item and note the folder name and library for citation.

### 3. Search Newspapers on Microfilm

Most small-town newspapers from before the 2000s are only on microfilm. Local
coverage of openings, zoning fights, runaways, arrests, and deaths often never
made it online.

- Check online first: your library card may give free access to
  Newspapers.com, NewspaperArchive, ProQuest Historical Newspapers, or
  NewsBank. [Chronicling America](https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/) is free
  but mostly covers older papers.
- If it isn't digitized, find which libraries hold the paper on microfilm.
  Search the paper's title in [WorldCat](https://www.worldcat.org/) or ask the
  state library.
- Narrow your dates before you scroll. Use known dates from lawsuits,
  corporate filings, NATSAP directories, or survivor accounts. Without an
  index, you're reading page by page.
- Ask if the paper has an index. Some libraries keep card or typed indexes to
  the local paper by subject or name.
- Microfilm can travel. Many libraries will borrow reels through interlibrary
  loan so you can read them locally.
- Save scans with the paper name, date, and page number in the filename, then
  add them to Zotero.

### 4. Search Special Collections and Archives

Special collections hold unique materials: personal papers, organizational
records, oral histories, and rare publications. A former staff member's,
journalist's, legislator's, or activist's papers may document a facility in
detail.

- Search [ArchiveGrid](https://researchworks.oclc.org/archivegrid/) for
  finding aids across thousands of archives. Try the facility name, founder,
  parent company, and state.
- Check likely holders:
  - State archives (legislative hearings, agency records, licensing files)
  - University special collections near the facility
  - County and local historical societies
  - Denominational archives for religious programs (e.g., Baptist, LDS,
    Catholic)
  - Archives of former reporters or advocacy groups who covered the industry
- Read the finding aid first. It lists what's in each box and folder, so you
  can request only what you need.
- Contact the archivist before visiting. Many collections are stored offsite
  and must be requested days ahead. Some archivists will scan a few folders
  for a fee.
- Cite precisely: collection name, repository, box number, and folder number.

### 5. Use Other Library Resources

- **City directories and old phone books:** confirm addresses, name changes,
  and staff names year by year. Many are on Ancestry (free at most libraries)
  or held in local history rooms.
- **Government documents:** [Federal Depository Libraries](https://www.fdlp.gov/)
  hold congressional hearings and agency reports, including hearings on
  residential youth programs. State libraries hold state legislative hearings
  and reports.
- **Dissertations and theses:** search ProQuest Dissertations & Theses through
  a university library. Graduate students sometimes studied specific programs.
- **Interlibrary loan (ILL):** borrow books, reels, and dissertations your
  library doesn't own. Ask at the reference desk or fill out the online ILL
  form.
- **Academic library guest access:** many public universities let visitors
  use databases on library computers, even without a student ID. Call ahead
  to confirm.
